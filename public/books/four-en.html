Story One · Creation

The Awaited Guest

The Word of God

My Story

When the sun goes down, the world could have simply gone dark, like a switch flipped off. Yet every evening the western sky is poured full of orange and violet. On the day of the first snow, grown adults crowd the window with the faces of children. What we feel in that moment appears on no list of what we need to survive.

Yet most of us live our days by the opposite rule. At eleven at night, one person compares lowest prices and star ratings, and another rewrites a cover letter, proving their usefulness on a few sheets of paper. The scale we use for things becomes the scale we use for people, and at night we turn it on ourselves. How much was I worth today?

A world poured out without counting the cost, and people who count every cent. What is this mismatch telling us?

Opening QuestionHave you ever asked yourself, “Why am I here?” When did that question usually come? (when choosing a path, when things weren’t working out, alone at night, after something big happened, and so on)

One Man’s Story

At the opening of Les Misérables, the French novel written over a hundred and sixty years ago, there was a door in the small town of Digne that was never locked, even at night. It was the door of Bishop Myriel. In the evenings, travelers no one else would take in sat at his table. The table asked for no proof. Even when supper was only soup, both silver candlesticks were lit, and the guest was given the same silver spoon as the host. The table said one thing: we had not come to beg a meal; we were the guest he had been waiting for.

His Story

1. A Room Prepared in Advance

Look at Genesis 1 and the order stands out. Light comes first, eyes later. Air comes first, lungs later. What is needed always arrives before the one who needs it. And at the very end comes the human being, the way a couple expecting a child chooses wallpaper, assembles the crib, and picks a name before the baby arrives. God made human beings in his own image, and the first thing he did after making them was to bless them (Gen 1:27–28). The first people received before they worked, and enjoyed before they repaid anything.

Looking CloserIf I were the traveler at that table where no proof was asked, would I feel at ease or uncomfortable? If the place of the first people, who received before they worked and enjoyed before they repaid, feels strange to me, why might that be?

2. The Signature Sets the Value

Almost every refrigerator has a child’s drawing on it, with a crooked sun, a dog with five legs, and the child’s name in shaky letters in the corner. No one at a market would buy that sheet of paper, but in that house it would not be traded for anything. Its value was set not by its usefulness but by whose hand it came from. The Bible calls us God’s workmanship (Eph 2:10). A product’s value is set by its price tag; a work of art’s value is set by its signature.

Psalm 139 tells when that signature was set.

As the room was prepared before the guest arrived, someone was waiting for me before I was born. So I am not an accident. I was not tossed by chance into a cold universe, and I am not a part to be thrown away once I stop being useful. I am one of a kind, made by God on purpose and with great love. The gospel begins with this God who made us in love.

Central Question“I am not an accident. I am a special being whom God made with great love.” Do I believe this? If some thought or experience makes it hard to believe, what is it?

3. The Joy of the One Who Made Us

In Genesis 1 the same sentence is stamped again and again: God saw that it was good. But on the sixth day, when he made human beings, the sentence has one more word: it was very good. The one who looked at light and sea and stars and called them good looked at people and called them very good. And that joy did not end with the first morning.

Question to Linger OnIf God rejoices over me with gladness and sings (Zeph 3:17), what changes in the habit of measuring myself by grades, performance, or reputation? Write down one place this week where I would like to set that scale down.

Our Story

This world is not a factory saving on costs but a room someone prepared generously while waiting for us. God counts us weighty and calls us with a song of joy.

Practice. In the next few days, set aside five minutes a day to look not at a screen but at the sky, the season, the warmth of a cup of tea, and say one quiet word: “Thank you.” Take your time thinking about whom you are thanking.

Carry this question until you open the next story: if this world was made so generously, why does living in it hurt so often?

Story Two · Fall

People Who Became Numbers

The Word of God

My Story

We live with many numbers: student ID and employee number, rank and score, years of service and job title. Called by numbers often enough, we start thinking of ourselves as numbers. When the numbers are good, our shoulders straighten; when they are bad, we would rather not face anyone. The numbers set the mood, and the mood sets the day.

If God counts us weighty, how did beings made so weighty come to think of themselves as so light? And in a world made so generously, why does it hurt so often?

Opening QuestionHave you ever finally gotten what you dreamed of and felt strangely empty? (after getting into school or landing a job, the first day with something new, the day after a promotion, and so on) What was left unfilled?

One Man’s Story

In October 1815 a drifter with a pack on his back arrived in Digne. He had stolen a loaf of bread for his starving nephews and nieces and got five years for it; each time he tried to escape, his sentence grew, until he had spent nineteen years in prison. Prison took his name and gave him a number: 24601.

Now he was free, and he had nowhere to go. The inns saw the yellow passport that marked him as a convict and said they had no rooms. When he tried to lie down in a dog’s kennel, the dog drove him out. As a last resort he knocked on the door that was never locked. Before he could say a word, an old voice inside spoke first: Come in.

His Story

1. Thirsty Far from the Spring

The Bible calls sin breaking God’s law. Its root goes deeper. Jeremiah gave it in two pictures: forsaking the fountain of living waters, and digging cisterns for ourselves that are broken and leak.

Someone who has left the spring is thirsty, and someone who is thirsty will dig any cistern. Henri Nouwen said we live believing three lies: I am what I have. I am what I do. I am what others say about me. Bank account, achievement, and reputation are the names of the cisterns we dig. Because they leak, they run dry again however much we fill them. This leaving began on the day the first people chose to become their own masters instead of trusting the One who gave (Gen 3), and that choice reached everyone (Rom 5:12).

2. You Are the Man

David, king of Israel, took the wife of one of his officers and, to cover it up, had the husband killed in battle. Then the prophet Nathan told him a story: a rich man with many flocks took the one little lamb a poor neighbor had raised like his own child, and served it to a guest. David burned with anger and shouted that the man who did this deserved to die. Nathan pointed at the king: You are the man.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, the Russian writer who spent eight years in the camps, wrote that the line dividing good and evil runs through the heart of every human being. David, who saw another man’s sin so clearly, finally saw his own, and his first words were a confession: “I have sinned against the LORD” (2 Sam 12:13, ESV).

Looking CloserDavid was furious at the rich man in Nathan’s story—“he deserves to die”—yet could not see his own sin. Why are other people’s sins so easy to see and mine so hard? Do I admit that I am included in the “all” of Romans 3:23?

3. A Door That Does Not Open from the Inside

Then can’t we just pull ourselves together and try harder?

A glass of clean water with just one percent poison in it cannot be drunk. That ninety-nine percent is clean is no comfort. Sin is not a stain on one corner of life but a poison that has seeped into every part of it, thinking, heart, and will. This is called total depravity.

And someone who has left the spring is not sick but dead. A cut flower in a vase looks lovely for a while, but the moment it was cut from the root, it was already dead.

The dead do not make themselves alive. God told Cain that sin was crouching at the door and that he must rule over it (Gen 4:7), yet in the very next scene Cain kills his brother. The command was right; the power to obey it was not in him. This state, in which we can neither overcome sin nor return to God by our own strength, is called total inability. These two terms show honestly why salvation has to come entirely from God’s side. I am not a little sick; I am a sinner. Admitting this is where the gospel comes in.

If the door will not open from the inside, the only way left is for it to be opened from the outside. Jacob had cheated his brother and was on the run. One night he fell asleep in a field with a stone for a pillow, dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven, and woke crying out, “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven” (Gen 28:17, ESV). That door opened not before a qualified person but before a man on the run. Centuries later, Jesus showed that the ladder Jacob saw was he himself (John 1:51), and he said:

Central QuestionHave I thought of myself as “a little sick,” or as someone “dead in the trespasses and sins” (Eph 2:1)? How does the way I see it change how I approach the gospel?

Question to Linger OnIf someone said, “Still, I’ve lived better than most people,” how would you answer using the glass of water with one percent poison in it? Have those ever been my words?

Our Story

We became numbers not first because of a few bad deeds, but because we left the spring and dug our own cisterns. And that door does not open from the inside. This heavy diagnosis is not meant to crush us. Only those who admit they cannot fix themselves hear the voice calling from outside.

Why does it hurt so often? Here is the first part of the answer. My pain is not simply the price of my own wrongs. But pain did not come because the world was badly made; it entered the world where the first people left the spring.

Practice. In front of one thing you have been wearing yourself out trying to fix on your own, stop before you try harder and say quietly, “I can’t do this by my own strength.” Notice what is left in your heart after you say it. The next story begins there.

Story Three · Redemption

The Silver Candlesticks

The Word of God

My Story

Anyone who came to America and looked for a place to live for the first time remembers. You have no credit history in this country. Whatever your life was back home, on paper you have proven nothing yet. Then someone writes their name in the guarantor’s line: a friend who settled here first, or an older person who has known you for years. That signature means one thing: if this person does not pay, I will.

There are people like that when we are wronged, too. When no one will listen however much we explain, someone steps forward and says, I know this person. I will answer for this person. With that one sentence, our words finally begin to be heard.

So a guarantee is no light signature. It is a promise that if the other person falls, I will fall in their place. In the last story we stood before a door that does not open from the inside. Sin is also a debt we cannot pay ourselves. Is there anyone who would write their name beside that debt? And could that person really pay it?

Opening QuestionWhen you need someone’s help, what are you usually like? (quick to ask, determined to do it alone to the end, hesitant because you hate to impose, and so on) Is receiving help you can never repay easy or hard for you?

One Man’s Story

That night, for the first time in his life, Valjean was treated as a person. He sat at a table set with silver spoons, was called “brother,” and lay in a bed with sheets. But just as a hand frozen for a long time hurts rather than warms when held near a fire, the warmth that touched a heart frozen for nineteen years felt like pain. Having forgotten how to receive, he stole the silver plates that night and ran.

The next morning the gendarmes caught him and dragged him back to that door. One word from the bishop and he would spend the rest of his life in prison. The door opened, and the bishop said, “Ah, there you are! But why did you leave the candlesticks? I gave you those along with the silver.” He put the two silver candlesticks into Valjean’s hands, sent the gendarmes away, and said, “Jean Valjean, my brother, you no longer belong to evil but to good. I have bought your soul.”

His Story

1. Who Paid the Price

The thief went free without paying. Did the price disappear, then? The bishop paid for the silver. This is what a guarantor does: the debt does not vanish; its owner changes. Of course, the illustration goes only so far. The bishop bore a loss, not a punishment in Valjean’s place. That morning is only a shadow pointing to the cross.

If a judge let a guilty person go free at no cost, we would call it not mercy but injustice. God did not look the other way at our sin. He sent his Son. The Son wrote his own name beside our debt and paid it with his own life. That is why the Bible calls Jesus “the guarantor of a better covenant” (Heb 7:22, ESV).

But the bishop did more than cover the theft; he added the candlesticks. God likewise did not only take our sin away; in the empty place he put his own righteousness on us. Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more (Rom 5:20).

Looking CloserWhy did the bishop not stop at covering for the stolen silver, but add the candlesticks too? What did Valjean receive that morning? In 2 Corinthians 5:21, sort out what went to Jesus and what came to us.

2. Open Hands

If the price has been fully paid, how does it become mine? A paycheck is earned, so we do not bow our heads when we receive it. A gift is made possible by the giver’s heart, so we bow. That is why a gift we can never repay is hard to receive. That is why Valjean ran.

Yet the moment many people hear the word faith, they take it as one more task. My faith is so weak; will it be enough? Imagine a bridge over a deep canyon. One person strides across; another crawls across on trembling knees. If the bridge is steel, both are equally safe. What carries us across is the strength of what our feet rest on.

The Bible has someone who crossed with trembling knees. On a stormy night Peter got out of the boat and walked on the water toward Jesus. But when he saw the wind, he was afraid and began to sink.

What saved him was not his faith but the hand that took hold of him. His faith was little, but that hand did not let go. Faith is open hands. Becoming empty-handed also means putting down what we held on to, the things we used to prove ourselves, and the very direction of living without God. This turning around is called repentance. Repentance is turning toward the Father who, while we were still a long way off, is already running to us (Luke 15:20). Even these open hands are given by the Spirit, who blows like the wind and opens the heart (John 3:8).

Here is the proof that the bridge holds. On the cross Jesus said, “It is finished” (John 19:30). Three days later the tomb was empty, and more than five hundred people saw the risen Lord at one time (1 Cor 15:6). That is why the disciples who had scattered became people who risked their lives to tell this news.

Central QuestionHave I ever doubted my salvation because my faith felt small? What do the bridge over the canyon and Peter sinking in the water say to that doubt?

3. You Belong to Good, and My Brother

What changes the moment these open hands take hold of Christ? The two things the bishop said are a shadow of the answer.

First, we are declared righteous. As a court pronounces a verdict of not guilty, God declares the person who believes in Christ righteous on the spot, because Christ’s righteousness has been put on him. And the one God declares righteous he sets apart as his own, as holy. Paul wrote to the church in Corinth that they had been washed, sanctified, and justified, all as things already done (1 Cor 6:11). The moment I believe, I am a holy person. This is called positional sanctification.

Second, we become children of God. To all who believe in his name he gives the right to become children of God (John 1:12). On the day we believe, our name changes. And into the heart of the one who has become a child, God sends the Spirit of his Son.

The Spirit comes to live in him and joins him to the body of Christ. Paul wrote that in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body (1 Cor 12:13). This is the baptism of the Spirit. It is not a second experience that a few receive after long waiting, but the beginning everyone who believes in Christ receives the moment they believe. What keeps being renewed for the rest of life is the fullness of the Spirit.

Both have already been done. They do not shrink when my faith is small and wavering. So before saving faith, the question is not how big my faith is. It is whom I believe.

Question to Linger OnThe bishop’s two words, “you belong to good” and “my brother,” point to which two things done for me the moment I believe (1 Cor 6:11; John 1:12)? On a day when my faith feels small, which of the two will I hold on to?

A Prayer in Answer to the Invitation

The price has already been paid, and the gift belongs to whoever holds out open hands. Jesus calls, “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink” (John 7:37, ESV). The only qualification he asks for is thirst. If your heart is ready, follow the prayer below with your eyes and confess it quietly within. For those who have long believed, it is a prayer that renews the confession of the day they first believed.

God,
I have come here thirsty.
I have lived away from you, digging my own cisterns with my own strength.
I confess that this was sin against you.
Please forgive me.
I believe that Jesus died on the cross in my place
and paid the full price of my sin.
I believe that he rose again on the third day.
I receive this gift with empty hands.
Now I turn around and welcome Jesus as the Lord of my life.
I am not my own now. I am yours.
In Jesus’ name I pray. Amen.

If you prayed this prayer. What to hold on to is not the size of your prayer but the promise of the Christ who received it: “whoever comes to me I will never cast out” (John 6:37, ESV). The church confirms and celebrates this confession together in baptism. Please tell a pastor of the church.

Our Story

If the bishop’s silver bought one man’s remaining years, the blood of Christ bought our eternity. Why does it hurt so often? Here is the second part of the answer. The price of our sin, the root of the pain, has been paid, and he carried even our griefs and sorrows (Isa 53:4).

Practice. In the next few days, tidy your room or desk once. Jesus said that one who has bathed needs only to wash his feet (John 13:10). Getting dust on our feet when we stumble does not cancel the bath.

Carry this question until you open the next story: if salvation does not depend on the size of my faith, what does faith mean after salvation?

Story Four · New Life

The Name Restored

The Word of God

My Story

On the first day of unpacking in a new place, the body arrived, but the heart did not arrive that day. Arriving takes a day; settling takes a long time. Faith is the same. The morning after receiving the gift whose price was paid, we usually wake up as the same person. I have been handed the key to a new house, so why do I still live by the habits of the old one? The last story asks this: how does someone who has arrived settle in?

Opening QuestionHave you ever arrived somewhere new in body while your heart took a long time to arrive? (a move, a new school, a new job, immigration, marriage, and so on) What made settling in hard?

One Man’s Story

On the evening he left Digne with the silver candlesticks in his arms, Valjean put his foot on a coin a passing boy had dropped and would not give it back. Only after the boy ran off crying did he realize what he had done, and that night he went back and knelt at the bishop’s door.

Eight years later, under the name Madeleine, he was the mayor whose factory fed hundreds. Then word came that an old laborer named Champmathieu, mistaken for Jean Valjean, was on trial. If he kept silent, he could live out his days as the respected Madeleine. After pacing all night, he stood up the next day in the courtroom at Arras.

“I am Jean Valjean.”

His Story

1. A Name That Is Never Canceled

Where did he find the strength to give up a fine false name and claim a dangerous true one? When the world called him 24601, there had been a voice that called him brother.

Peter also received a name. It means rock. Yet on the night Jesus was arrested, by the charcoal fire in the high priest’s courtyard, he denied knowing him three times. The risen Lord lit another charcoal fire on the shore of Galilee and waited for him, and three times he asked, “Do you love me?” Each answer was met with a charge: Feed my sheep.

The standing we have received is not canceled by failure, and the life we live grows on top of that standing. We do not grow in order to become children; we grow because we are children. The Lord praised those who, with but little power, did not let go of that name (Rev 3:8).

Central QuestionJesus did not bring up Peter’s three denials; he gave him a charge: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17). Do I believe that even where I have collapsed, my name as God’s child is not canceled? What difference does that belief make in getting up again?

2. The Planted Seed

In the third story we saw that salvation does not depend on the size of our faith. When the disciples asked him to increase their faith, Jesus said that faith like a grain of mustard seed was enough.

But a seed is not there to stay a seed.

A seed lying on the soil and a seed planted in the ground are the same size. The seed on the soil only looks up at the birds flying over; the planted seed grows into the tree they nest in. Between the two lies being buried in the ground. Jesus said that unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24).

On the evening he stood on the coin, Valjean was a seed on the soil. He had received, but he had not yet been planted. On the night he knelt at the bishop’s door, the seed fell into the ground. On the day he spoke his own name in court, that seed put down roots under his own name.

A seed holds the whole life of the tree from the start. The moment we believe, we are already set apart as God’s own, as holy (positional sanctification). But a seed has to grow to become a tree. Growing in holiness for the rest of life to match that standing is progressive sanctification. Salvation does not depend on the size of our faith, but how much of the salvation we have already received we enjoy and live out on this earth follows how far that seed has grown. And grown faith overcomes the world (1 John 5:4).

Looking CloserOn the very evening he received the candlesticks, Valjean put his foot on a boy’s coin. He had received—so why was he still the same? What changed on the night he knelt at the bishop’s door? Is there an area of my life where I have received but not yet been planted?

3. While We Sleep and Wake

How does a planted seed grow? A farmer scatters seed and then sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows, he knows not how (Mark 4:26–28). What makes it grow is not the farmer’s diligence but the life in the seed. God removes the heart of stone, gives a heart of flesh, and puts his Spirit within us (Ezek 36:26–27).

That is why Jesus spoke of growth in terms of staying joined more than of effort.

The Word and prayer, worship with baptism and the Lord’s Supper, and community are the places where we stay joined to him. That does not make us spectators. Paul put the call “work out your own salvation” and the declaration “it is God who works in you” side by side in one sentence (Phil 2:12–13, ESV).

4. Shade, and the Day the Pain Ends

A grown tree casts shade. Peter, who had collapsed in the courtyard, proclaimed the name he had denied before thousands some fifty days later, and before long people hoped that even his shadow would fall on their sick. Valjean, given the chance to execute Javert, the man who had hunted him all his life, cut his ropes instead. People who had hidden their names became people who cast shade.

The pain of this world has a day when it ends: the day Christ returns and makes all things new. For those in him, that day is the day the verdict already given—no condemnation—is confirmed before the whole world.

Until that day, the pain we have passed through is not thrown away; it becomes shade. The place I most wanted to hide becomes a place where someone comes to rest.

Our Story

We were made as awaited guests, became numbers when we left the spring, and received a gift with open hands. And we have heard that the name we received is not canceled by failure, and that a planted seed grows into someone’s shade. This is how someone who has arrived settles in.

Practice. In the next few days, remember the name of one person you usually deal with only by role or title, and greet them by name. And tell one person the scene from the four stories that has stayed with you longest.

If you haven’t prayed yet. Open again “A Prayer in Answer to the Invitation” in the third story. If you tell a pastor of the church, they will rejoice with you.

Question to CarryWhen a mustard seed grows into a tree, the birds of the air come and nest in it. What bird—someone or something—did I once keep off my branches out of fear or discomfort, but can now let nest there?
