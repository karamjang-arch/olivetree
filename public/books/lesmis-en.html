Introduction

Three Voices

Each story in this book circles the same ground three times. First our own story asks a question. It is the story of people who shop late at night, spend their days on a scale, and lie awake looking at the ceiling. Then Victor Hugo’s novel Les Misérables lives that question out in one man’s life. From beginning to end, the novel follows a man who lived in prison as a number and slowly becomes a person again. Last of all, the Bible answers. At the opening of each story there is a short piece in which a character from the novel tells us something in their own voice, and at the close there is a place to stay awhile alone, called “My Story.” Without a question, a story becomes someone else’s business. Without a story, an answer turns into a lesson. For that reason the order does not change until the book ends. Each story has three places, named Our Story, One Man’s Story, and His Story. And at the end of each story, one more place is left empty. It is My Story, and this book will not fill it.

Les Misérables holds a special place in these pages. It is not an illustration used for a moment and put away. From the first story to the last, it walks beside us. We follow one man, Jean Valjean, as he meets the gospel, comes apart, and is built up again, a little more in each story. So if a story stops while the tale is still in motion, that is not an ending. It is a comma, a pause before the next story, and the next story picks up the walking from where we stopped.

This book hopes for one thing. Two thousand years ago a story about one man, Jesus, was told. A little over a hundred and sixty years ago, in a novel, it became the story of another man. I hope it can become ours today: the story of Jesus, becoming our story.

Story One · Creation

The Awaited Guest

Opening — Madame Magloire

Imagine the housekeeper of Bishop Myriel’s house in Les Misérables, looking back on that house years later.

The sums in that house never came out right.

All my life I have hated to waste anything. I counted the silver every time I polished it. When I saw flowers blooming where the potatoes might have gone, I counted meals in my head. At that door that never locked, my heart sank every night. I found the old gentleman who gave everything away exasperating. I thought him a man who knew nothing of the world, and I thought that without me his household would have gone under long ago.

But here is the thing. My own accounts always balanced, and somehow they were poor. His accounts never balanced, and somehow his table was rich. Even on nights when supper was only soup, he would have me light both candlesticks. I lay awake guarding what we had. He gave it all away and slept like a child.

So lately I wonder. Might that waste know something I don’t? Might there be a world that shows itself only when we put down the counting?

Our Story · The House Called the Universe

An Encore for the Sun

When the sun goes down, it would have been enough for the world to go dark, like a switch flipped off. If survival were the point, a world in black and white would have done the job. Yet every evening something suspicious happens in the western sky. No one has paid for it, and still orange and violet are poured across that whole enormous canvas without a thought for the cost.

Science explains a sunset as air scattering light, and the sweetness of fruit as a plant’s strategy for spreading its seeds. Both explanations are true. But nowhere in them is a reason why that color should stop someone on the walk home and make something in the chest ache a little. Music is the same. A few vibrations overlap, and a person weeps. When the first snow falls, people in an office drop their work and gather at the window. Snow blocks the road and chills the shoulders, yet grown adults press against the glass with the faces of children. Survival would have been fine with black and white, with no scent, with no sound. This fullness we feel is a surplus. It appears on no list of functions. It sits outside the bill. To some people it is a leftover that evolution happened to leave. To others it is a clue that this world was made with more care than it needed.

As children, we were experts in surplus, experts in waste. We pocketed useless stones like treasure, picked the puddles on purpose to step in, and asked for a story a hundred and one times after hearing it a hundred. The overflow of the world and the overflow of a child’s heart fit each other well. Becoming an adult may have meant handing those eyes in at the door. We learned to walk around puddles and to keep walking past sunsets, and at the end of it we were handed our credentials as grown-ups.

A little over a hundred years ago, London was home to a grown man who never accepted those credentials: a large, laughing newspaper columnist named G. K. Chesterton. A young atheist named C. S. Lewis, who would later write the Chronicles of Narnia, read his books and came away admitting that an atheist who wants to stay one had better be careful what he reads. Chesterton made it his life’s work to keep wonder alive into adulthood, and he found it in a child’s play rather than in a laboratory. A father swings his child up into the air, and the child laughs until breathless and shouts at once, Again! Again! The grown man’s arms give out around the thirtieth throw, but the child is ready on the hundredth to laugh as if it were the first. For an adult, repetition is fatigue. For a child, it is an encore. Chesterton then imagines something strange. What if the sun does not rise each morning out of a wound-up clock’s habit, but because someone calls to it every morning, with endless delight, to do it again? In Orthodoxy he put it this way: it is possible that God says every morning, “Do it again,” to the sun. What if the millions of daisies in a field are the work of a hand that shapes each one as if for the first time and has never once grown bored? He wrote that God may have “the eternal appetite of infancy; for we have sinned and grown old, and our Father is younger than we.” The one who tires of repetition is not heaven. It is us. We went stale with age, and the one who made us may be younger than we are.

Chesterton’s eyes teach us to see the world as a gift. But wonder is an attitude, not an address. However dazzling the wrapping, the wrapping alone cannot tell us whose name is written in the recipient’s box. That is why the first thing a person does on finding a parcel at the door is turn it over and read the label. Is this mine? Whether the parcel is addressed to the whole world, or to one person inside it, to me, is a different matter. Wonder can bring us as far as a question. It cannot answer it. Chesterton, too, cannot name the one the parcel is addressed to. He leaves the question standing and steps back.

So far the world looks like a gift box packed past the brim. Not what was needed, but more than was needed. Not just enough to survive on, but enough to enjoy. And the hands that received this box have learned the opposite motion.

The World on a Scale

It’s eleven at night, and someone lies on a bed sliding a thumb up the phone screen. The purchase is a phone charger. The thumb hops between three or four sellers looking for the lowest price, checks the star ratings, and scrolls through dozens of reviews. Only after adding in shipping does the person tap Buy, and falls asleep with a small relief at having chosen well. We call this carefulness being “rational.”

That carefulness has changed even our language of praise. Walking out of a restaurant, we say “not bad for the price” instead of “delicious.” In front of something good, where an exclamation should go, we put “great value.” The confirmation that we got back more than we paid is what satisfaction means in our age.

The carefulness did not stop with objects. By day the same thumb plays lecture videos at 1.5 times the speed, disposes of a two-hour film with a fifteen-minute summary, and swallows a book in three lines. The map app shows the shortest route first, and we pick even our lunch by star rating. Once time had a price tag, everything we did came with a quiet question: did I get my money’s worth? The last thing to step onto that scale was a person.

That same night in another room, a young man is revising a personal statement. This is what I achieved, and this is how I would benefit your company. It is the polite business of proving one’s usefulness across a few pages. This paperwork follows us all our lives under changing names. The report card becomes the résumé, the résumé becomes the performance review, and the performance review becomes the retirement forms. The thumb that picked a phone charger makes the same motion on screens where people are picked. We compare careers, check reputations, and weigh salary against efficiency. At the door of the interview room, someone rehearses a one-minute self-introduction. One minute: the time allowed to a person who has lived nearly thirty years to summarize what they are worth. And at night, the eyes turn to the mirror. What was I worth today?

We sum up the grammar of this life in one phrase: earn your keep. Our existence has been billed, and we are supposed to pay it off with work. We trade the phrase like a joke, but a serious thing lies under the joke. What if the day comes when we can’t pay? We know that the questions flying across the holiday table (Got a job yet? When are you getting married?) are not asking for news. They are asking for results. A retired father’s evenings wear that serious face: a man who drove himself all his life with the words “earn your keep,” with nothing left to pay, speaks less at dinner while the television stays on late into the night. A young man had another face on the night the rejection email arrived. He said he felt returned in full, like a package.

After a night spent proving, something feels strangely empty. Where does the rest of me stay, the part the forms can’t hold, the me with no line on the list?

A world poured out without counting, and people who count down to the last cent. The grammar of the gift and the grammar of the scale come apart every day inside one person.

One fact puts a crack in the logic of the scale: value does not always come from the thing itself. Nearly every refrigerator has a child’s drawing stuck to it somewhere. A crooked sun, a dog with five legs, a mother taller than the house. In the corner, in letters still clumsy, the child’s name. Put that paper on the market and no one would buy it, yet in that house it is not for sale at any price. They won’t give it up when they move. Years later, after the child has grown, it turns up at the back of a drawer, kept as it was. The paper is not precious. The drawing is not brilliant either. What set its value was not the drawing’s usefulness but the name in the corner: whose hand it came from. Then perhaps the value of a person should be asked about somewhere else. Not what have I accomplished? but whose name is on me? I’ll fold that question away for now. Still, the belief that the scale is all there is has already begun to crack.

Someone will shrug, of course. That’s how the world works, they’ll say. If you don’t prove yourself you get pushed aside, so what are you supposed to do? That’s fair. None of us made this grammar, and no one gets off this train alone. But we can still ask separately whether this grammar is the whole of the world. Whether there is truly not one room the scale can’t reach.

The Door Left Open

With that question in hand, let’s look into a small room in a house.

A couple expecting a child go into that room every weekend. The child hasn’t come yet, but the room comes first. The two argue at length over the color of the walls. They argue with perfect seriousness over pale cream and white, two colors that look identical to anyone else. The husband spreads the instructions on the floor and assembles the crib, and because one screw is left over he takes the finished crib apart and starts again from the beginning. The wife tapes a list of name candidates to the refrigerator door and says one aloud each time she walks by. In a few years a drawing will hang in that spot with that name in the corner. They hang a mobile over the crib. They know a newborn’s eyesight is blurry and will not remember this time. They hang it anyway. They change the curtains, stick guards on every corner of the furniture, and at night turn off the light and stand there a long while, judging how dark the dark is.

In the husband’s wallet is an ultrasound picture, a blurry shape not yet quite a face, and he has taken it out more times than he can count. Even when the room is done, the couple do not shut its door. They leave it a little open so that they can look in at any time. They know how to compare prices. They comb the baby-gear sites more thoroughly than anyone. But they search in the opposite direction: they look for better, not cheaper.

Put this room in a ledger. Return on investment: unmeasurable. The recipient’s awareness: none. Plan to recover the cost: none.

Yet the couple’s faces are strange. They don’t look like people who lost out. They look like someone carrying flowers home, like people who found the baby shoes at half price and still wonder whether they should have bought the better pair.

Those flowers will wilt in a few days. We buy them knowing that, and perhaps because of it. If lasting were the point, we would have chosen the silk ones. To a dog who earns no salary we give a name, we don’t hesitate at the vet’s bill, and on the day that short life ends, a grown adult weeps. Before a baby who does nothing but sleep, a person becomes the richest in the world. Love isn’t ashamed of spending that doesn’t balance. It goes looking for places to pour itself out. The crime our age fears most is waste, but love spends without counting and does not call it waste.

Someone may feel a chill at this scene, someone who never had such a room. That chill tells us something: we already sense that someone should have prepared such a room for us too.

A Single Coin

Each of us began life in a room like that, or at least began by longing for one. And the room is not only the nursery. If we open our eyes a little wider, we can see a much larger room, one that was furnished before any of us was born. The Greeks called it kosmos, a word that meant both “order” and “ornament,” and we still hear it in the word cosmetics. The same question applies. Which does this world resemble? A factory saving on costs, or a room decorated while waiting for a child?

By a factory’s grammar, the answer is settled. Anyone who has toured a factory knows. On a production line, what is managed is error, not beauty, and in the estimate there is not a single line for spending without a reason. So a factory finishes only what can be seen. Plating is applied only thick enough not to wear off, packaging is lovely only until the moment it is torn open, and the inner parts no hand will touch are left rough. We know what the back alley of a glamorous showroom looks like. Only as much as it takes to sell: that is the law of cost. By that law the inside of the world, where no one ever looks, ought to be rough. Yet a single snowflake under a microscope was already forged into six-fold symmetry before any human eye reached it. In valleys no foot has crossed, flowers bloom in full color. In caves no one has entered, crystals grow for tens of thousands of years, keeping their angles. The finish goes all the way into the places no one is watching. Whoever finished this world did not skip the places no one sees.

One of the strangest documents left by twentieth-century physics is a list of the conditions that had to line up for life to exist. What makes the list strange is that many of the people who compiled it were not looking for signs of design. They were measuring. And every value they measured stood at the edge of a cliff. The strength of gravity. The strength of the electromagnetic force. The distance between the sun and the earth. The fact that water, when it freezes, becomes less dense and floats. The angle at which the earth’s axis is tilted. The share of oxygen in the air we breathe is on the list too. Higher, and a small spark would turn into a wildfire. Lower, and breathing itself would be labor. Every item stands on the point of a needle. A little more gravity, and stars would burn too hot and go out early. A little less, and they would never have lit at all. A little different, and the carbon that life is made from would never have formed, or the oceans would have frozen from the bottom up.

Hugh Ross, who earned a doctorate in astronomy and later became a Christian apologist, paints a picture of the odds that a single one of these conditions would land right by chance. Cover the whole North American continent in dimes, piled up until the stack reaches the moon. Then build a billion more continents like it. Mark exactly one dime among all those mountains with a red dot and mix it in. A blindfolded person reaches out one time and must pull up the red dime. That is the scale of the odds. The number that tunes how fast the universe expands, which physicists call the cosmological constant, is far harsher. One part in ten followed by a hundred and twenty zeros. It means the value did not slip off by even the hundred-and-twentieth digit after the decimal point. Physicists themselves call this one of the most baffling numbers in the history of science.

It is not only the numbers. That the universe can be read in numbers at all is strange. The work of science is, in the end, to write the world down in equations, and astonishingly, the world takes the dictation. Planetary orbits, the bending of light, the inside of the atom: they all can be written as equations. Four hundred years ago, Galileo said that the great book of the universe is written in the language of mathematics. If it is written in a language, it has a grammar. If it has a grammar, someone built that grammar. And a book means someone had something to say. Of course this alone cannot tell us who the author is, or what the author meant to say. But we can all look together as far as this: that the world resembles a well-formed speech more than it resembles noise spilled at random.

A well-known objection follows this list around. It says there are countless universes, and we simply woke up in one where the conditions happened to fit. A lottery winner is not amazed that someone won, the argument goes, since someone had to. I won’t try to settle that hypothesis here. But whichever explanation we choose, one thing stays the same. Before our first breath, the mix of the air had already been set, and the warmth of the seasons already tuned. The room was finished when we arrived.

Still, physics has a limit to what it can say. A scale can measure precision, but it cannot weigh a heart. However detailed the blueprint, nowhere on it is written whether the designer loves the tenant. What we can read from the list of fine-tuning is that this universe was not made carelessly. Whether that precision was affection for someone is another story entirely. Standing before the mountain of coins, we marvel, but marveling and welcoming are not the same word. The more we read the list of precision, the more astonishing it is, but astonishment alone will not let us move in and live there. To be a home, a place needs the assurance that someone was waiting for me. Between design and hospitality there is still a river to cross.

At that riverbank, tonight before you go inside, I hope you’ll stop for a moment and look up at the sky. The city lights may have left only a few stars. Even so, some of that starlight set out before any of us was born. Set the distance that light crossed beside the exactness of those numbers, and it becomes dizzying. Next to that dizziness comes a feeling that is hard to explain, something like longing. It makes no sense to long for a house we have never been to, and yet this feeling resembles homesickness. Between the two feelings, some think of dice and some think of waiting. Either way, the question will not let us walk around it.

Is this universe a factory or a room? Is that precision an accident or a preparation? And if it is a preparation, who was it prepared for?

Beside that question, if we’re honest, stands another. If this world is truly a room decorated this carefully for someone’s arrival, why does living in it so often hurt? Where in this room are the long nights in a hospital ward written down, and the person who didn’t come home, and the crying that reaches us from far away? We won’t answer today. A hasty answer is one more wound to a person in pain. We’ll carry this question with us to the end of the fourth story.

One Man's Story · The Bishop's House

If there is an answer, it may begin in a house whose door is never locked. Someone did spend his life longing for such a house and wrote about it: the French author Victor Hugo, and his novel Les Misérables, published more than 160 years ago. That house appears in the novel’s opening pages.

Let’s begin with the door.

Digne is a small town in the southeast of France, at the foot of the mountains where the Alps begin. In one of its narrow streets there was a door that was never locked at night. By day or night, a push would open it. It was not always so. To learn how that door forgot how to lock, we have to go back to a day in 1806.

That year a new bishop came to Digne. In a small town, a bishop’s arrival is a great spectacle, and for days people had been guessing at the procession. How many attendants? How many carriages? How deep a purple would the robes be? What arrived was an old man in a worn cassock and two women. His name was Charles-François-Bienvenu Myriel. He was well past sixty, and with him came his sister Mademoiselle Baptistine, ten years younger, and Madame Magloire, a housekeeper who was quick with figures and full of small worries. Their belongings were even fewer than expected: a few crates of books and one box of silverware. The crowd that had come out to greet them peered behind the carriage and was surprised first of all that this was everything.

The bishop’s palace in Digne was the finest building in town. It was a stone mansion with high-ceilinged reception rooms, long galleries, and an old garden, where bishops before him had hosted guests and held banquets for more than a hundred years. And right beside its wall stood the hospital. A low, narrow, dark building.

On his third day in office, the bishop went to visit the hospital. The director came out to the door to meet him.

“How many rooms do you have?” the bishop asked. “Five or six, Your Excellency.” “And how many patients?” “Twenty-six.” “The beds are pushed together.” “There is no room.”

The answers went on: no room to move patients who were recovering, none to separate them when a fever spread. The bishop looked over the wards without a word. Light from a narrow window showed the gaps between one bed and the next.

When he had finished looking, the bishop turned to the director. His voice was courteous, but the face was that of a man whose mind was already made up.

“Director, I believe there has been a mistake. You have twenty-six people in five or six small rooms, and the three of us live in a house that could hold sixty. You are living in my house, and I am living in yours. Let us trade back.”

The next day twenty-six beds were carried over to the palace, and the bishop moved into the house that had been the hospital. Where banquet guests had strolled for a hundred years, recovering patients now walked the galleries with a hand on the wall. In each high-ceilinged reception room the beds spread out with space to spare, and in the old garden chairs were set for those who came out to sit in the sun. The town whispered for a while. The story that the new bishop had given away the best house in town within three days came up once at every dinner table. The man himself showed no sign of thinking it a great thing. He did not look like someone mourning a loss. If anything, he looked like a man who had changed into shoes that fit. After that, whenever he passed his old house, the bishop slowed his steps. When patients sunning themselves in the yard recognized him and tried to rise, the old man took off his hat to them first. It was a scene in which the one who had given up the house bowed to those who received it, and it was hard to tell which of them was in the other’s debt.

The bishop’s salary was fifteen thousand francs a year. The evening of the move, he sat at his desk, spread out a sheet of paper, and wrote down the year’s household accounts.

So much for the seminary. So much for the care of prisoners. For the children of men in jail, for orphans, for a mountain village with no school, for the ransom of people jailed for debt. The list grew longer and longer down the page. What was strange was the face of the man writing. It was not the face of someone recording expenses. It was the face of someone writing a list of gifts, someone adding names to the invitations for a feast. Only at the very end of the list did a short line appear. Myself: one thousand francs. Of fifteen thousand, fourteen thousand went to other people’s names. In today’s terms, he gave away ninety-three percent of every paycheck.

Madame Magloire took a peek at the paper. She said nothing and went to the kitchen. But that day the sound of dishes in the kitchen was louder than usual. She had a point to make, too. The three of them had to live on the bishop’s thousand francs plus his sister’s small pension. If Madame Magloire haggled a penny off at market, the bishop had somehow already found where that penny should go. The one who saved and the one who gave away lived under one roof, and strangely, the household never went under.

People who saw the bishop hand something to someone all said the same thing. It was not the gesture of a benefactor; he did not hand it down from above. He gave like someone paying back a debt long put off, with the face of someone thankful to be allowed to give. In the old man, the people of Digne saw for the first time that there is a way of giving that leaves the receiver’s back unbent.

A few years later, this happened. The provincial council set aside another three thousand francs for the bishop’s carriage and travel. The respectable people of the town said that now even that house with the hospital smell would have a carriage. The carriage never came. Only a few more lines were added to that sheet of paper. The bishop took the carriage allowance and went without a carriage. He rode a mule, or walked. His diocese was in the mountains, and to visit the remote villages he had to cross several passes where bandits were said to lurk. When the gendarmes offered an escort, the bishop declined. To those who warned him of the danger he answered, smiling, that there was nothing to take from an old man and a mule. And he did set out on a mule and cross the pass. In the villages beyond, people came out with lanterns to meet him. The mountain people did not forget the bishop who crossed such a hard road on his own feet. The old man did not forget them either, and the next year, and the year after, he crossed the same pass.

In that house, money did not stay long. Whatever came in flowed out toward the names outside the door. Yet among all those who came and went through that house, no one called it poor.

Things like this cannot be hidden. Before long the people of the town had set aside his long, formal name and called him by only the middle one. Bienvenu. In French, it means “welcome.” The long name was on his papers; this short one was earned by the years he had lived. The children called him that first, in the alleys, then the grown-ups followed, and finally people could not tell who was meant by any other name. A man for whom a greeting became a name. The name the townspeople picked for him became the nameplate on that house.

Behind the house that had been the hospital was a garden no bigger than a palm. The ground was divided into four plots. Madame Magloire grew vegetables in three of them. In the last one, the bishop planted flowers. In the mornings he would crouch in that one plot. He grudged the hours at his desk no more than the hours in the soil, and he raised peonies, hollyhocks, and wildflowers without a name, side by side, with no ranks among them. He was a bishop with dirt under his fingernails.

Madame Magloire was quick with figures. She knew exactly how many winter meals one row of potatoes would yield, and how many meals the peonies were costing by sitting in that spot. So one spring day, behind the back of the bishop watering the flower bed, she finally asked. Why did he take such trouble over things that couldn’t be eaten?

The bishop straightened up. A hand with soil on it smoothed a bent blade of grass. The skin at the corners of the old man’s eyes creased.

“Madame, you are mistaken. What is beautiful is every bit as useful as what is useful.”

He picked the watering can up again and added:

“Perhaps more so.”

Madame Magloire looked for something to say, gave up, and went back to the kitchen. That year too, the potatoes came in plentifully from three plots, and the flowers bloomed uselessly in one. On summer evenings the scent of those useless things drifted over the wall and down the street. No one had paid for it. Passersby often stopped in front of that wall. A woman from next door took home a few blooms, she said to put by the bed of her sick mother. That garden seemed to know that things you cannot eat sometimes raise a person up.

In the evening, a stranger often sat at that table. A peddler, someone begging until night fell, a traveler no one else would take in. A wet coat steamed by the fire, and beside it a strange man warmed frozen hands. Such evenings came to that house often enough. What the bishop did for the person who pushed open the door was always the same. He gave up the best chair by the hearth and had a place set at the table. He did not even ask the name first. Where had the man come from, what had he been doing to end up here, could he earn his keep? That table asked for no proof of any of it. What appeared on the table was usually soup and bread and nothing more. But on that plain table, one luxury the old man had never managed to give up glittered.

It was silver. Six place settings and a large ladle, and two heavy silver candlesticks. The candlesticks were an inheritance from a great-aunt who had died long ago, and these pieces of silver were all that he had brought from his old house. The bishop did not hide this weakness. With a smile, like a man confessing a sin, he would say that the habit of eating with a silver spoon is the hardest one to break, even in old age. Madame Magloire polished that silver every day. She polished it counting the pieces. To her, the silver was the last property the house had left. To the bishop it was something else. A man like him would have let such things flow away long before. His silver was not locked in a strongbox. It was put to work on the table, for the most unknown stranger.

The evening table was laid with that silver whether a guest was coming or not. And on evenings when a guest sat down, Madame Magloire brought out both candlesticks and lit them. She did so even when supper was only soup. The two flames reflected again and again in the polished silver, and for that evening the poor table shone as brightly as any banquet hall.

Imagine it. A person who has been turned away from one door after another all day long sits down in that place. One bowl of soup, two candles. A silver spoon laid at his place. The same bowl as the host’s, the same bread, the same light. Anyone would understand what that table is saying. You did not come here to be fed. You are the guest we have been waiting for.

On the morning a guest left after a night’s stay, the old man came out to the door to see him off. He took the guest’s hand in both of his and told him to take care, and to stop in again whenever the road got hard. A sincere person can be heard in the voice. Some who walked out of the alley with that farewell at their backs, after walking a long while, put a hand in a pocket and found a few coins that were not theirs, and stopped. No one had seen when he put them there.

The door of that house still had no lock. It is a faraway story for us, who change the entry code with the seasons and judge the footsteps outside through the peephole before we open. At first people took it for something that had been missed in the confusion of the move. After years passed and the lock was never fitted, everyone understood that it was no oversight but a decision. A priest from a neighboring parish once suggested it gently: with the world so unsettled, surely he should lock up at night. The bishop only smiled. But in the margin of a book he had been reading, a sentence remains in his hand. A doctor’s door must never be closed, and a priest’s door must stay open.

When the night grew late, the old man went out into the garden. It was an old habit before bed. He stood on the narrow path between the potato patch and the flower plot, arms folded, and looked up at the sky for a long time. The nights over Digne were thick with stars. Mademoiselle Baptistine sometimes watched him from the window. In the dark, only the white hair floated faintly, and over it the stars crowded. His sister never disturbed her brother. Whether that time was prayer, or admiration, or conversation for the old man, she never asked. No one knows what he thought in those hours. No record remains. But one thing seems clear. What does that sky have to do with me? This is what sleepless people ask beneath the stars, and the old man did not ask it. He looked at the sky with the face of a man who had already received his answer. Then he went inside, past the door that never locked, and slept like a child. The more a house has to guard, the shallower the sleep. In Digne, the man who slept most deeply was the one who locked up least.

Nine years passed that way. On an evening in early October 1815, not long after sunset, a rumor went through the town. A drifter with a knapsack and a face hard as rope had come in through the gate, every inn had refused him, and now he was wandering the streets. Some said he had come out of prison. The rumor traveled faster than any door could be shut. That night in Digne, the sound of shutters closing and bolts sliding home rang down every alley earlier than usual. The whole town was locking up, every door but one. Madame Magloire, setting out supper, had picked up the rumor. The talk of locks came up again. With the world like this, what if anyone at all pushed their way in? Tonight at least, the door ought to be blocked with something. In the morning she would send for the blacksmith. Behind her voice hovered the six place settings of silver she had polished every day.

The bishop sat by the fire warming himself, and whether he heard her or not he gave no reply. Madame Magloire’s speech trailed off unfinished.

A knock came at the door. A fist pounding on wood, three times, thick and heavy.

The candlelight wavered. The flames trembled on the polished silver. Madame Magloire’s hand rose to her chest, and Baptistine looked at her brother without a sound. The bishop was looking at the door. A door he had not locked for nine years. In the voice of a man who had been the same all his life, without a moment’s hesitation, he said:

“Come in.”

Why does one person’s open door mean so much to another? What is a person worth, that a door should open for him? To find the answer, let’s go back three thousand years.

A Small Boy’s Big World

The nights are long on the hills of Bethlehem. The flock sleeps, the campfire sinks, and a single boy is awake. He is the youngest of eight brothers, so the night watch is always his. His brothers sleep in the village, his father’s sheep sleep on the hillside, and the boy has nothing to do but prick up his ears now and then at a wolf’s howl somewhere beyond the dark. The night air is cold, the smell of wool is warm, and now and then a shooting star scratches a line across the dark and vanishes. On such nights the boy lies down on his back in the grass. And then the whole sky pours down.

We don’t know the night sky from before there were city lights. A sky where “many stars” falls short, where there seems to be more light than dark, where the Milky Way looks as though it flows like a river with a sound. Under it, the boy lies as a single dot. He starts counting the stars and gives it up. Beside his knee are a sling and a staff, and in his hand is a harp. He began playing for the sheep, and somewhere along the way he has begun playing for the sky.

The Bible does not hide what place this boy held in his family. Later, when the prophet Samuel, who anointed kings with oil from a horn, came to the house and asked to see all the sons, the father lined up seven and stopped (see 1 Sam 16:10–11). He had not thought to call the youngest, who was out in the field. A child who did not count in the family’s head count. A child so light he needed no scale. And that child held to himself, every night, something bigger than anyone else had: the whole sky.

And on one of those nights, a question rises in the boy. Years later the grown man would put those nights into song, and this is what he sang.

“When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him?” (Ps 8:3–4, ESV).

What is man. We know this question. It is the same question we asked at the end of the night when we compared every price before sleep, the same one we asked under the ceiling after a day of proving ourselves. What does that sky have to do with me? Am I the guest who was awaited, or a tenant who drifted in?

But the boy’s night differs from ours in one respect. His question received an answer. And we know where that answer is written. It is the story he heard from childhood, the story his people had set down on the first sheet of the scroll. Now we are going to open that first sheet together. What deserves our attention is not how many days it took, but what was made first and what was made later: the order.

God’s Hospitality

Genesis 1 is laid out like a builder’s log. For the first three days, God separates. On the first day he separates light from darkness, on the second the sky from the waters, and on the third the sea from the dry land. These are the three days in which the frame of a formless world goes up. For the last three days, God fills. On the fourth day he hangs the sun, moon, and stars in the place of the light he separated on the first day. On the fifth he releases birds and fish into the sky and sea he separated on the second. On the sixth he places animals on the land that appeared on the third. Each day has a partner. The sentences are built so that the first day faces the fourth, the second faces the fifth, and the third faces the sixth. It is a structure in which chaos becomes order and emptiness becomes fullness. Raise the frame, make the rooms, bring in the furnishings: anyone who builds houses knows that sequence. It means this was no story written down as it happened to roll along. It is a story with the blueprint in front of it.

People have argued over this first chapter for a long time. How long is a day? In what way was it made? I have no intention of judging that argument here. Wherever we stand, we can keep our eyes on what the text works so carefully to show. The order.

Light comes first, and the eye later. Air comes first, and the lungs later. Water comes first and fish later, and fruit comes first and the mouth later. What is needed always arrives before the one who needs it. And at the end of that order, at the very end of the sixth day, comes the human being.

We have already seen this order somewhere. Pick the wallpaper, assemble the crib, hang the mobile, choose the name ahead of time, and only after all that does the child come. Genesis says the universe was made that way. Before the human being first opened his eyes, the room was complete. The person on that first morning had done nothing at all. He had never sown a seed, dug a channel, or hung a single star. And yet before him the sky and the earth and the morning table had already been set. The table in Digne, laid in silver whether or not a guest ever came, looks like a distant echo of this first table. The human being received before working and enjoyed before repaying. The first person in the universe began life as a guest.

There is one more thing about the order. The human being was made at the end of the sixth day, and on the seventh God rested (see Gen 2:2–3). So the first whole day a human being ever met was not a day of labor but a day of rest. A calendar where the first feast comes before the first day on the job. A being whose résumé has rest written on the first line where achievements would go: that is the human being Genesis draws.

At the close of nearly every day of creation, the same sentence is stamped like a seal. God saw that it was good. The same seal was pressed on the light, on the sea, on trees bearing fruit, on creatures with wings. In the first chapter alone it appears six times. A maker who looks back again and again at what he has made and is pleased: you will not meet him in a factory trimming costs. But on the sixth day, after he made the human being, the seal changes. “And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good” (Gen 1:31, ESV). Very. In the whole account of creation, the only world to receive that adverb is the world with people in it. The sentence is aimed at no audience. That evening there was no one in the universe yet to hear the delighted cry. What the record keeps is a craftsman’s sound before a finished work, a nod to himself, words that slipped out because he was satisfied. Every room was good, and the house with its guest inside was very good.

The Image of God

One word is set into the record of the sixth day. Image.

“So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them” (Gen 1:27, ESV).

Let’s go back for a moment to the time when this word first reached its readers. The kings of the ancient world could not travel to every corner of their empires themselves. So they set up their images wherever they went. A statue in the middle of a plaza was not decoration but a declaration. It was a public document cut in stone: this land lies under that king’s rule. The people bowed before the king’s statue in place of the king’s face.

Genesis speaks in the language of that very age. But the direction is reversed. God did not carve stone. He set up a person who breathes, walks, and laughs as his image. In the place where kings put statues, God put human beings. And not one king in one palace, but male and female, the one who plows and the one who draws water, the youngest boy who did not count in the household. God arranged it so that meeting a person is the same as standing before the image of the King.

In that age, defacing a king’s statue was treated as laying a hand on the king himself, and it was paid for with a life. Read that grammar in reverse and here is what it means. A scale that weighs people carelessly, a calculation that cuts a person down to a few documents, is laying a hand on the image of the King. Long before the phrase “human dignity” existed, the first chapter of Genesis set down the ground of that dignity. And an image comes with a task. As a king’s statue shows the king’s rule on his behalf, human beings were set in place to govern this world in the Creator’s way: dividing, filling, tending, caring. That is why the “subduing” took the shape not of trampling but of working the garden and keeping it (see Gen 2:15).

The word order in the next verse deserves attention too. “And God blessed them” (Gen 1:28, ESV). Only after that comes the charge to have dominion over the earth. Before giving them work, God gave them a blessing. It is like a new hire welcomed with a blessing before a single day’s work, with no record of accomplishment. A blessing received not because of anything done, but before anything was done. In the grammar of Genesis, being always comes before achievement.

The second chapter of Genesis changes the lens. The screen that had lit the whole universe narrows to a single garden, and as it narrows, the senses deepen. The smell of earth, the sound of a river, the green that differs from tree to tree. If the first chapter is a builder’s log, the second is the record of a housewarming. And when we reach the description of that garden, a familiar voice seems to speak over it. “And out of the ground the LORD God made to spring up every tree that is pleasant to the sight and good for food” (Gen 2:9, ESV). Pleasant to the sight is written before good for food. A table where color is recorded ahead of calories. By the logic of survival the order should have been reversed, but Genesis writes it down without a flicker. If we set beside that order the answer the old man in Digne, watering can in hand, gave his housekeeper (what is beautiful is as useful as what is useful, perhaps more), the two sentences seem to light each other.

God’s Song

Folded deep inside the Bible is a small prophetic book called Zephaniah. It has only three chapters, and most readers do not even know the name, and most of the book is dark with warnings of judgment. But at the very end of that dark book is set a sentence in which the Creator’s heart seems to open a hand’s breadth wider.

“The LORD your God is in your midst, a mighty one who will save; he will rejoice over you with gladness; he will quiet you by his love; he will exult over you with loud singing” (Zeph 3:17, ESV).

He will rejoice over you with gladness. He will exult over you with loud singing. That means he sings. The one who made the universe with a word is so full of joy over human beings that he breaks into song. That sentence is written in the Bible. The first people to whom Zephaniah told this song were God’s people, whom God was about to gather again. And the song God sings over his people is held out as an invitation to everyone standing at the door today. The line set between has a different texture: he will quiet you by his love. Between the shouting joy and the bursting song sits one line of love that does nothing but gaze, in silence. We know the long silence of a parent leaning over a sleeping baby. That face, the richest in the world before a baby who does nothing but sleep: Zephaniah says that this is the look God wears when he regards a human being. What the newspaper columnist in London a hundred years ago groped toward by intuition from the children’s Again! Again!, the guess that some tireless joy might be turning the world, was not a guess but a text. The one who calls on the sun every morning for an encore sings aloud over human beings.

This song has a flip side. Anyone who knows even a little of a parent’s love knows it. The greatest reward a parent receives is not a child bringing something home to lay at their feet. It is the child laughing without a crease of worry inside that love, eating well, sleeping well, enjoying life without fear. For a parent, that is the feeling of having gained the whole world. If God is the same, then the first glory we can give him may not be some grand accomplishment but a day well received. The sentence Paul left points exactly that way. “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God” (1 Cor 10:31, ESV). At the head of the list of glory, the Bible names eating and drinking first, before any sacrifice or achievement: a table. A step stopped before a sunset, an evening meal eaten to the last bite, a day that ends in laughter with someone we love. As enjoying a gift to the full gives the greatest joy to the one who gave it, a life that receives and enjoys a world poured out without counting, like a child, becomes the delight of the one who made that world. We have been invited to live our days to that song.

Now back to the hills of Bethlehem. The boy lying in the grass, who asked what is man?: the song writes the answer he received on the very next line.

“Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honor” (Ps 8:5, ESV).

At the start of the song, the boy called the heavens the work of God’s fingers. Fingers. Not the labor of rolled-up sleeves, but work done with a fingertip. If the size that left us dizzy is only the scale of a finger to the Creator, then what he poured his whole heart into lies elsewhere. And the song says what that is, in the next line. It says he crowned us. Long before the shepherd’s youngest son wore a king’s crown, he knew what the song says: every human being wears one, for the simple reason of being human. That vast sky does not erase me. It is the size of the crown placed on me.

The Hebrew word translated “glory” in that line is kavod. Its original meaning is a surprise. Heavy. For the Hebrews, glory was weight before it was shine. What is light blows away in the wind. What is heavy holds its place. To glorify someone is to treat them as weighty. A heavy thing is not handled carelessly. The more precious a thing, the more likely we are to carry it in both hands. To say that God counts human beings weighty is to say that God lifts you with both hands.

And this weight does not stop with you alone. One writer said that this truth changes the whole way we treat people: we have never met an ordinary person, not once. The person whose shoulder bumped yours on the subway today, the tired face behind the counter at the service window, the family member who sits at the same table every day: none of them is background passing by. Each is a being of glory whom the Creator counts heavy, a person set up as the image of the King. The reason we have lived weighing one another on scales may be that we lost the eyes to see the other person’s weight. When this truth opens those eyes, something else takes the place where we used to put a price on people. It is the careful awe we feel before a weight we cannot treat lightly.

We were people who spent our days on a scale. And that scale always weighed us light. The weight of a few documents, the weight of a one-minute self-introduction, the weight of one line of accomplishment. It was a weight that gets pared down and drops toward zero when our usefulness runs out. The retired father who loses his words at the dinner table, the night a single rejection email made us feel returned in full: all of it happened on this scale. Because we dread growing light, we spend our lives piling things on. We pile on a career, a title, other people’s approval. What we piled on is taken off one day, and the scale sinks again. The verdict of Genesis points exactly the other way. Before you were born, the room was finished. Before you had done anything, the blessing came. Before you set any price, the seal very good was already stamped.

The youngest son, who did not count in the household head count, believed that verdict. Even on the day his brothers were called forward seven times and he alone was left in the field, he did not forget the song. Under the night sky he had learned that there is another scale, whatever the world’s scale made of him.

The Bible has a confession that came out from under such a sky. Jacob, running after cheating his brother, fell asleep in an open field with a stone for a pillow. That night the sky opened, and when he woke he cried out. “How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven” (Gen 28:17, ESV). The house of God was not a house found by a qualified person. It was found by a man on the run. Centuries later, Jesus told those around him that they would see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man (see John 1:51), a name Jesus used for himself. With those words he said that he himself is the ladder between heaven and earth.

In the next story we will meet a man who laid his body on a cold stone bench in the night.

Tonight that sky is still over your head. The city lights may have left only a few stars. That is all right. What the boy in Bethlehem saw, in the end, was not a count of stars either. Under that same sky, we can now read the verdict he was given.

The answer the boy received was this. You are not an accident. You are what the Creator had in mind. You are not a commodity valued by price but a song bursting from the joy of the Almighty. So I hope you will hear this one sentence tonight under that sky. God counts you weighty. He holds you dear above everything in the world.

My Story — Before You Close the Book

You have come through the first story. Three voices circled the same ground three times. Our story, lived on a scale, asked. An old man’s story, the story of a man who never locked his door, lived that question out. And the first chapter of Genesis answered. The three pointed in the end to one thing. This world is not a factory but a room that someone decorated with care, and you are the guest who was awaited in it. God counts you weighty.

Before moving on to the next story, stay here a moment with three questions. Carry those three voices over, this once, into your own voice. It isn’t an exam where you get the answer right. It’s laying the story you have read over your own life.

People Who Became Numbers

The last story closed with a declaration: God counts you weighty. So how does someone that weighty end up thinking of themselves as a number? Let’s hear the question first in one man’s voice.

Opening — 24601

Imagine a man who has walked out of prison after nineteen years, telling us about his first days.

Can this face be mine?

Today, for the first time in nineteen years, I saw my face in a puddle. The eyes have sunk deep, like an animal’s. The mouth has forgotten how to smile. I stared a long while before I understood who was looking back. It was me. The man who was seized over a loaf of bread one winter at twenty-five and let out only well into his forties. The man who once shared his bread with his sister’s children.

Prison took my name and gave me a number. 24601. For nineteen years that number, not my name, was what anyone called me. At first I ground my teeth at the injustice. Then I hated the world. In the end I simply became the number. Forgetting a name does not take long.

Now I am free. Strange, though: the door is open and I have nowhere to go. My passport is a yellow paper, and people shut their doors when they see its color. On this first night of freedom I learned that nowhere in the world is there a place for me.

I want to ask. Is this strange face mine? Or did I lose myself somewhere, a long time ago?

Our Story · The Prison We Built

This story opens with a sentence from Jeremiah. It is a lament, and God is the one lamenting: a people who left the spring and dug cisterns that leak. Let’s begin by seeing how near that lament sits to our own lives.

The Me Who Became a Number

Start with a picture of Sunday night at eleven o’clock. The week is nearly over, and plenty of people lie awake with their phones. The screen holds next week’s schedule in a neat column, with the list of everything still undone beneath it. A thumb drifts, without deciding to, to everyone else’s news. Someone won an award. Someone changed jobs. Someone posted photos from a trip. Scrolling through them, something in a corner of the heart goes quietly heavy. Where am I, exactly? Is this pace okay? Right up to sleep we measure ourselves against other people, and we fall asleep holding an answer sheet that never finished being graded. Tomorrow morning a fresh one will be handed out.

A moment ago we stood before a declaration: God counts you weighty, and you are the guest the room called the universe has been waiting for.

Some of you may have felt uneasy reading that. “Counted weighty” can sound like something said about someone else, because we do not count ourselves so weighty. We weigh ourselves light, and often. By what we got done today. By how other people rate us. By this quarter’s results. To a person who steps onto the scale every day, “You are weighty” arrives as something strange long before it arrives as comfort.

Right in that discomfort, a question we have put off for a long time lifts its head. How did a being made this precious come to treat itself so lightly? How did the awaited guest come to feel like a number?

If the word “number” does not sound like an old story, it may be because we live with several. A student ID and an employee number. A class rank and a GPA. Years of seniority and a title. The numbers are convenient, since they let other people place us at a glance. But convenience has a price. Hear yourself called by a number often enough and you begin to think of yourself as one: the me who ranks this high, the me who earns this much, the me who sits in that seat. When the number is good, the shoulders go back. When it is bad, standing in front of people becomes unbearable. The number sets the mood, and the mood sets the day. By then the name has stepped back and the number stands in front.

A comfort comes attached to numbers, and it is comparison. If my number beats yours, I relax for a moment. I’m better off than that person; at least I haven’t fallen apart as far as they have. We soothe ourselves this way all the time. But there is a trap in this comfort. Picture a glass of clean water. Stir in one percent poison, and the water cannot be drunk. That ninety-nine percent is clean is no comfort at all. Purity is not a matter of proportion. This is why the relief of being less broken than other people cannot save us. We keep measuring how far things have gone wrong, while the real trouble is that something has already been mixed in.

Before we look for an answer, I hope you’ll keep in mind the voice from the opening: the man who saw his own face in a puddle after nineteen years and found it strange. His question, is this strange face mine?, does not belong to him alone. Now and then we are caught by the same question in front of a mirror, with no idea when we came to have this face or where we began to lose ourselves. From here we will trace the path of that losing, because if we know where we took the wrong turn, the way back comes into view. First, a map drawn by a philosopher of our own day.

Invisible Bars

Byung-Chul Han, a Korean-born philosopher who writes in Germany, calls today’s society the “burnout society.” His observation goes like this. Societies of the past were disciplinary societies, which held people in with walls and watchtowers. What was forbidden was clear, and someone outside stood ready to punish anyone who crossed the line. Our age has pulled down those walls. Nobody tells me “don’t” anymore. Instead everyone tells me the opposite: you can. You can do more.

At first that sounds like liberation. The bans are gone and the possibilities are open. But here is the reversal Han points out. The exploiting master has not disappeared. Master and slave have moved into a single person. The overseer driving me now lives inside me. Nobody asks me to work late, and I do. Nobody is grading, and I rank myself. Even in rest I worry about falling behind. The person in the disciplinary society wanted to get out past the wall. The person in what Han calls the achievement society has no wall to get out of. He built the prison himself and swallowed the key.

It is a strange prison, when you think about it. The door stands wide open, and nobody leaves. No guard keeps watch, yet everyone carries bricks at their post. The prisoner of old knew he was locked up, and so at least he could dream of freedom. Today we do not even know we are locked in, because we believe we are staying by our own choice. What is frightening about this room is that the bars cannot be seen. Bars you cannot see cannot be broken either. There is nothing in sight to break.

What makes this self-exploitation so cruel is that it does not feel like exploitation. It feels like diligence. It feels like self-realization. So we lay the bricks one by one and call it self-improvement. That is why Han names the sickness of our age depression and exhaustion. With no oppressor outside, there is nothing to resist, and a person whose only target for hatred is himself finally snaps against himself.

We know in our bodies why this diagnosis is accurate. Anyone who has squeezed a life into a few lines of a personal statement, or had a few minutes in an interview room to prove their usefulness, knows how deeply that scale has been built into us. The night of a failed exam, when it felt as if not a score but your whole self had been sent back to the store. After a presentation, replaying the other person’s face and feeling your own value rise and fall. An old friend’s cheerful “So what have you been up to?” sounding like a quiet ranking. Those are the moments when we touch the wall we built with our own hands. The wall was never far away. It was always within reach of our fingertips.

A Mind That Cannot Be Alone

More than 350 years ago, a French mathematician had already looked into the root of this busyness. Blaise Pascal wrote that all of humanity’s unhappiness comes from one thing: not knowing how to stay quietly in a room alone. We keep doing, going, and connecting because if we stopped, we would have to face the self waiting there, and that frightens us. Achievement is a splendid hiding place. A busy person has no time to look at himself. Perhaps we are not running for fear of falling behind. Perhaps we are running because we are afraid of the face we would see if we stopped. Like the man who saw his face in a puddle only after nineteen years, we put off looking at our own, again and again.

I’d like to set one more voice beside Pascal’s. Henri Nouwen, the spiritual writer best known for The Return of the Prodigal Son, summed up the blueprint of this prison in a few sentences. We live, he said, by believing three lies. I am what I have. I am what I do. I am what others say about me. The point, in my own words, is that we build our identity on these three pillars, and all three stand on shifting sand. What we have can be lost. What we have done can be forgotten. What others say changes from morning to evening. A person built on them feels his whole existence shake whenever any one of them does.

Build on what I have, and I seem to shrink when my bank balance does. Build on what I have done, and one failure seems to erase everything I was until then. Build on what others say, and one harsh word costs me a night’s sleep. The three pillars look as though they hold me up. In fact they hold me hostage, because they are me, and they are all there is of me. So a person like this forgets how to receive what is given free. Hand them a gift and they think first of how to repay it. Praise them and they start asking whether they earned it. To be loved as they are, to be welcomed without having proved anything: those words sound to such a person like a foreign language. The meaning is clear enough. The body cannot take it in.

The more we have run toward achievement, the more finely this lie is etched into us. Each time we passed through a gate, a good school, a good first job, a good position, we felt relief, but the relief did not last. The next gate was already there. The ladder of ability has no top. Climb one rung and there is another above it, and the fear of sliding down if we stop clamps around the ankles. So on the ladder there is no resting. It is not that we never learned how. Rest feels like falling. For a person like this, even rest becomes work: we rest well so that tomorrow we can run better, and so rest acquires a goal. Even a trip has to leave something behind, and even a hobby has to turn out useful someday. One by one, the things we do for no reward, only because we love them, drop out of our lives. When every hour is an hour for something, there is no hour left for the self that exists for nothing. At that point what confines us is no longer a room. It is the whole day.

A Door That Does Not Open from the Inside

So far we have read the records of the brightest eyes of our age looking into our prison. Han showed its structure. Nouwen pointed to the lies that led us to build it. Both are accurate. But an accurate diagnosis does not always carry us as far as the exit, and here we owe each other some honesty.

Han’s prescription is to step back from the life of the achievement-subject and return to a contemplative life. The direction is right. But who is to do the stepping back? Me, again. Telling someone who built this wall to walk out of it on his own is like telling someone who swallowed the key to take it out on his own. Nouwen’s prescription is to drop the three lies and stand on a true identity, but the diagnosis alone does not tell us where that true identity comes from. A diagnosis gives a sickness its name. Knowing the name does not heal the sickness.

To measure how deep the illness goes, I’d like to return for a moment to the garden in Digne. The bishop’s flowers could send their scent over the wall and into the lane on summer evenings because their roots were in the soil. Cut one and put it in a vase, and what happens? For a few days it looks, if anything, tidier and lovelier. We change the water. We set it in a sunny window. But we know the flower was finished the moment it was cut from its root. Wilting is a matter of time, and the splendor in the vase is death put off for a while. There is a sense in which this is what the Bible means by death: not the event of the breath stopping, but the state of being cut off from the source of life. A person can still smile, work, and achieve on the outside, with roots sunk nowhere.

If that diagnosis sounds excessive, try one honest test. We cannot decide even the most basic matters of life for ourselves. Could you make yourself an inch taller tonight? Could you tell your heart to skip a single beat? The heart keeps working while we sleep, but it does not wait for our approval. Can a person whose being alive at all is not his own doing reconnect a broken life by his own strength?

Sick and dead are not the same. The Bible does not describe someone who has left the source of life as sick, but as spiritually dead. For the sick, prescriptions and willpower help. For the dead, the finest doctor in the world is no use. A prisoner can at least dream of escape, but a flower in a vase has no legs to walk out on. What is needed is the reattaching of a cut stem to its root, and no human gardening has ever managed that.

Genesis 4:7 points to this exact spot. It is the first place in the Bible where the word “sin” appears. God told Cain, the eldest son of the first man, that sin was crouching at the door, with its desire for him, and that he was to rule over it (Gen 4:7). Yet in the next scene Cain led his brother out into a field and killed him. The command was right. The power to obey it was not in him.

Here is the heart of the matter. The key to this prison is not on the inside. It is a wall we built ourselves, so our own strength is already part of the wall. Even the struggle to escape by trying harder becomes one more task that the achievement-subject sets for himself. A struggle to get out that only thickens the wall. Then one possibility remains. If the door will not open from the inside, it has to be opened from the outside: not by me, who swallowed the key, but by someone who holds it.

None of this says to stop trying. Effort is precious. But the person who tries to batter down a prison with effort and the person who walks out through a door already open do not walk the same way. The first pushes against the wall with clenched teeth. The second lifts his head toward the voice that called him. What we need may not be a stronger push against the wall. It may be that voice from outside.

A Russian who came out of the camps left behind a sentence years later: the line dividing good from evil runs not between nations but through the middle of every human heart. For now I will only set that sentence down. Why it is our story will come clear later in this chapter.

So what we need may not be a better self-help book. It may be someone who will open this door from the outside. If such a thing ever happened, what would it look like? Perhaps like this: late at night, before a person to whom every door in the world has closed, one door that was never locked swings open.

Such a night did happen, two centuries ago, in a French town. Back, then, to the story we paused. The man who lived nineteen years as a number, the one who saw his face in a puddle a moment ago, now takes us into that night.

One Man's Story · Where Is My Home?

Let me pick the story up again at those three knocks.

The candle flickered, Madame Magloire’s hand rose to her chest, and the bishop said, “Come in.” The door opened, and a shadow darker than the night stepped across the threshold. A man with a soaked knapsack on his back and a face unwashed for days. Who he was, and how he came to knock that late at the unlocked door of a strange town, is what we have to tell now. For that, we need to wind the clock back to that afternoon.

The man who walked into Digne had walked all day and eaten nothing. His feet were blistered, and the knapsack on his shoulder felt like stone. After days on the road he had reached a strange town wanting only one thing: a roof, and a night’s sleep. When the sun went down he went into an inn. He had money. A little pay from nineteen years of labor was in his pocket. The innkeeper welcomed him at first, then saw the passport he held out, and his face changed. A yellow paper. The passport issued only to ex-convicts. The innkeeper said every room was full, rooms that had been empty a moment before.

It was the same at the next inn. The yellow paper did the same work at every door. A door would open, then close the moment the paper came out. At one place a man looked down from a window and shouted at him to go. Worse than the closed doors was the rumor. Let it get around that a convict had slept at your inn, and other guests would stop coming. So no one ever asked his name. The yellow paper was his name. People looked at the paper, not at the man holding it.

Night deepened. A few days out of prison, he was learning what freedom was. A man who had been locked up for nineteen years could now go anywhere. But he could stay nowhere. In prison there had been a place to sleep. Outside, there was not even that.

By the road he saw a doghouse. His worn-out body tried to crawl inside. The dog in it barked furiously and drove him out. When he understood that even a dog refused him, he backed away thinking: I have become something less than a dog. Even a dog has a home, and I don’t have so much as a doghouse. Nineteen years earlier, going into prison, he had never imagined he would come to this. All he had done was steal a loaf of bread, and the years had whittled him down to something worse than a dog.

He lay down on a stone bench in the square before the cathedral. The night wind was cold. An old woman passing by saw him there and pointed to a small house across the square, telling him to knock on that door. He did not believe her. With a whole town turning its back, what house would take in someone like him? Still he got up, because he had nowhere else to go. A light was on. Through the window he saw people gathered at their supper. He hesitated a long time before that warm light. They will throw me out anyway. What is one more time? With that thought, he raised his fist and knocked.

But before he could open his mouth, an old voice inside spoke first. Come in.

The door opened.

He froze where he stood. He had been about to say who he was. I am an ex-convict. Nineteen years of hard labor. Here is my passport. He had said it at every door, and every door had closed on those words. This house opened before he could say them. The bishop did not ask to see the yellow paper. He did not ask where the man had come from. He simply led him to the table, had another place set, and asked Madame Magloire to light the rest of the candles.

He would remember that evening for years.

Still, he could not hold it in, and he told them who he was. I am Jean Valjean, an ex-convict, nineteen years in the galleys. The bishop’s face did not change. Instead he said, “This is not my house. It is the house of Jesus Christ. This door does not ask the one who enters whether he has a name, but whether he has a sorrow.”

Soup was served, and bread. On that plain table the silver caught the candlelight and shone. A silver spoon was set before him, too. A man who had eaten for nineteen years from wooden bowls with iron spoons now had a silver spoon matching his host’s. The bishop treated him as a guest. No, as more than a guest. The word he used for him was brother. It was the first time in nineteen years he had heard it. The bishop did not scold his worn face or look askance at his ragged clothes. He received him as if a long-awaited guest had finally arrived. Mademoiselle Baptistine, the bishop’s sister, quietly filled his glass, and Madame Magloire, unable to hide her disapproval, still set a warm plate in front of him.

A warmth filled the room. Brother. In prison the word for him had been a number, and in the streets it had been a curse. A kind word from a human mouth, aimed at him, was something he had gone without for too long. So that one word sounded foreign to his ear. Like someone who suddenly hears his forgotten mother tongue in a faraway land, he went blank before it for a moment.

At that moment, something inside him collapsed.

We tend to think that love melts a heart. What happened to him was the opposite. The welcome hurt him.

To see why, let us step back from this evening and trace the nineteen years behind him. Without them, we cannot know what fell apart in him tonight.

He was twenty-five. His sister’s children were starving. In a winter when seven children shared one scrap of bread, he broke a bakery window and stole a loaf. The price of that loaf was five years. Then, shut away and sick with worry for the children outside, he tried more than once to climb the wall. Each attempt lengthened his sentence. Five years became nineteen. The arithmetic that began with one loaf of bread swelled like that.

Over those nineteen years he gradually forgot he was a human being. The guards called him by his number. The prisoners called each other by number. No one treated him as a person. For the first years he held on by outrage: how does one loaf become nineteen years? He ground his teeth at the world. For the next years he held on by hatred, getting through each day on the strength of hating the whole world that had done this to him. In the last years even the strength to hate ran out. He simply became a number. A number that woke, labored, and fell asleep at night, with its feelings dried up.

A man like that came out into the world after nineteen years, and the world confirmed what prison had taught him. You are not a human being. The inn said so, the street said so, even the dog said so. He took those words in without a fight, because he had believed them for a long time already. When the world treated him as a beast, he was, if anything, relieved. A beast does not need to be loved. A beast does not need to repay anything.

Then this house shook that comfortable belief.

The bishop treated him as a human being. No, as a brother. He gave him a silver spoon and the best seat, and received him without asking where he had come from. Before this welcome he was only confused. He had learned how to live as a beast, and he had long ago forgotten how to be treated as a man. The part of him that could receive love had gone numb. So this strange warmth came to him not as comfort but as pain. When a hand that has been frozen for a long time is suddenly held near a fire, it does not feel warm. It hurts. Warmth reaching a long-frozen heart was pain.

Late in the night the household went to bed. The bishop gave him a bed against the wall. Clean sheets and a soft pillow: a human being’s bed. For nineteen years what he had lain on was a damp plank. How long it had been since he lay in a bed like this, he could not even count. But sleep would not come. In the dark his eyes kept turning to one spot, the cupboard that held the silver he had noticed at supper.

Here is something to ask honestly. Why did he want to steal it?

It was not hunger. He had eaten his fill only hours before. It was not lack of money, either. His pocket still held the pay for his labor. The real reason he could not take his eyes off the silver was not only that it was valuable, but that it kept saying something he could not bear. You are a person. You are a brother. You belong beside this silver spoon. All night the silver whispered it, and he could not stand the whispering. If it was true, the past nineteen years had been far too unjust. If it was true, he had to become a man again, and to become a man meant to be able to hurt again. Staying a beast was less painful. To become a man was to face the nineteen lost years: the youth taken from him, the children who must have gone hungry, the time that would never come back. Rather than bear that pain, he would sooner stay a beast.

So he decided to steal it. Perhaps it was not theft but flight. Flight from the hand that treated him as a person. A struggle to pull away from the hand love held out, to go back to the only world he knew, the world of beasts. The moment he stole the silver he would be a convict again, and no one calls a convict brother, and then he would not have to hurt again.

Before dawn he rose without a sound. He opened the cupboard, lifted out the silver basket, and swept the silver into his knapsack. The empty basket he flung over the garden wall. The silver that had caught the candlelight again and again now slid into his knapsack in the dark. Silver polished for a guest had become loot in that guest’s pack. The same silver that had lit the table last night now rattled on the back of a thief running through the dark. Last night it had shone in the name of welcome. This dawn it was heavy in the name of betrayal. He went over the wall and ran into the night, his back turned on the house that had called him brother, running with all his strength.

But the world had not forgotten the yellow paper. The next morning gendarmes seized him. When they opened his knapsack, the silver spilled out. An ex-convict, a yellow passport, a knapsack full of silverware: a case with nothing left to ask. The gendarmes dragged him to the owner of the silver, to have the stolen goods identified and to send him straight back to prison.

So the man was dragged back, this time in the grip of gendarmes, to the door of the house he had fled. A gendarme knocked. It was the door he had knocked on the night before, and it was about to open. Last night it had received him as a brother. This morning he stood before the same door as a thief. A gendarme’s hand gripped his collar. He could not lift his head. He had no courage to face, as a thief, the bishop who had called him brother the night before. Now the bishop needed only to say one word to the gendarmes: yes. That silver is mine, and that man stole it. With that one word the man would spend the rest of his life in prison. And the word was the plain truth.

When the door opens, what will the bishop say?

His Story · You Are the Man

Someone Else’s Sin

Let me hold the novel here, one breath before the door opens, at the scene of the man seized by gendarmes and dragged to the house he robbed. What the old man will say we leave for the next story.

In the meantime there is something to ask. As you read that story a moment ago, where were you standing?

Most of us, probably, were standing beside the man. We looked into his nineteen years and tried to understand why he stole. Some of us, perhaps, stood a little way back and judged him: after such kindness, to steal the silver! Either way, we were in the place of those who watch him, the place of an audience. He was the one on stage, and we watched from our seats. The audience’s seat is comfortable, because what happens on the stage is not our business. We shake our heads at criminals on the news, grow impatient with the foolishness of characters in novels, and say of another person’s mistake, how could anyone do that? As long as we sit in judgment of others, we feel far from the dock. It is a safe distance.

Three thousand years ago, someone sat in a seat exactly like it. A king.

At that time David, king of Israel, was at the height of his life. As a boy he had brought down a giant with a single sling stone. After years on the run he had become king, and now the nation was at peace on every side. The shepherd who sang to God under the night sky had become master of a palace. He is the boy we met in the first story, the child who lay under a sea of stars and raised his question to the one who made him. Over the years he had taken a nation into his hand. He had known God that closely, which is why his fall is all the more unexpected. Yet at that height, he collapsed.

It was the season when kings go out to war. David sent his men to the battlefield and stayed in the palace himself. Late one afternoon he rose from his couch and walked on the roof, and he saw a woman. She was bathing, and she was beautiful. She was Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, one of his officers. The king had her brought to him, and she became pregnant. To cover it up, David sent her husband to the most dangerous place on the battlefield to die. Then he took the widow as his own wife. Being king made it possible. No one could accuse a king. David believed he had buried the matter. For a time, nothing seemed to happen.

The Poor Man’s Lamb

Soon after the child was born, a prophet named Nathan came to the king. To point at the king’s sin to his face was to risk his life, for this was a man who had killed to cover it. But Nathan did not begin with a pointing finger. Instead of rebuking the king, he told him a story. Like a petitioner coming for judgment, he poured a sad case into the king’s ear.

There were two men in a town. One was rich, and one was poor. The rich man had flocks and herds beyond counting. The poor man had nothing but one little ewe lamb. He raised it like a child. It ate what he ate, drank from his cup, and slept in his arms at night. To a poor man like him, that lamb was not property but family. It was as good as a daughter. And because it was the only one, it was also everything he had.

One day a traveler came to the rich man. The rich man had to feed his guest. But he was loath to take from his own many sheep and cattle. So he took the poor man’s one ewe and prepared it for his guest.

Hearing this, David was furious. He shouted at Nathan that the man who did this deserved to die, and swore it by the life of the LORD. Because he had shown no pity, he was to repay the lamb four times over. There was real anger in the king’s voice. He could hardly stand the rich man who had taken the only thing a powerless man owned. It was right anger. The trouble was where it was aimed.

The king’s anger was justified. The rich man in the story was cruel, and the king’s wish to punish him was right. At that moment David sat in the place of a judge who hears of another’s sin and rages righteously. On the stage stood that wretched rich man, and David was in the audience, handing down a verdict on him. It was the seat from which we watched the man who stole the silver.

At that moment Nathan raised his hand toward the king. He spoke the heaviest sentence in the Bible.

“You are the man!” (2 Sam 12:7, ESV)

The Mirror Turned Around

One sentence turned everything over.

That wretched rich man was no stranger on a stage. He was the man who sat in the judge’s seat handing down the verdict: the king. The rich man with flocks and herds beyond counting was a king with many wives. The poor man who lost his only ewe was the officer who lost his one wife. The stinginess of taking what belonged to another to feed a guest lay exactly over the king’s sin, which took another man’s wife and life to feed his own desire. The king had pronounced a death sentence on himself.

Nathan told the king no new sin; David knew exactly what he had done. He turned the verdict the king aimed at others back toward the king himself. He turned the mirror. The king saw his own face in the glass he had been using to look at other people. Like the man who saw his face in a puddle after nineteen years and found it strange, the king must have looked at his own face, in that instant, like a stranger’s.

Here we can name the Russian we set down earlier: Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn. He spent eight years in the Soviet camps and put that experience into a book, The Gulag Archipelago. He could easily have called those who imprisoned him evil. Yet the sentence he drew from that hellish place was unexpected: the line dividing good from evil runs not between nations but through the middle of every human heart. David’s line had first lain outside himself. That wretched rich man was the bad one, and he, the righteous king, was judging him. Good on this side, evil on that.

Nathan moved the line into the middle of the king’s heart. The judge and the one who needed judging were the same man. What Solzhenitsyn learned in the camps was the same. He had thought a line was drawn between himself and the wicked men who locked him up, and he found it ran through his own chest. Evil was not someone out there. It ran through the heart of every person, himself included. In the dark of the camps, Solzhenitsyn found again, in his own language, what Nathan said to a king three thousand years earlier. He saw where the line runs, but he could not tell us how to erase it.

So Nathan’s sentence was not aimed only at one king three thousand years ago. The same finger points at us, who watched the man with the silver, who have such sharp eyes for other people’s faults. You are the man. We are always ready to judge someone on a stage, but the Bible turns the mirror of judgment around and lights up our faces. As long as we sit in the seat of judging others, we can never be free. While we count other people’s sins we cannot see our own thirst. Only when the mirror comes around and shows my face do I learn that I too need healing. Only a person who admits to being ill goes to the doctor. Jesus said the same: a doctor is for the sick, not for the well (see Mark 2:17).

Thirsty People

So what is this thing called sin?

We usually think of sin as rule-breaking: crossing a line we were told not to cross. That is not wrong. Sin does break God’s law. But David’s story shows that the root goes deeper. David already had much. He had a kingdom, a people who loved him, and a God who had held him since his shepherd days. He lacked nothing. Yet he took the one thing another man had. A man who already had more than enough felt thirst.

The prophet Jeremiah gave this thirst a name, as God’s lament. My people have done two evils, God said: they have left me, the fountain of living water, and they have dug cisterns for themselves, hollowed-out tanks for storing rainwater, and broken ones at that, which can hold nothing (see Jer 2:13). The biblical words behind “sin” (Hebrew chata, Greek hamartia) hold a surprising picture: an arrow that missed its target. Before sin is a list of bad deeds, it names an aim gone wrong. Fire a rocket at the moon with the launch angle off by a single degree, and it passes thousands of miles from its target, through empty space. A small error at the start becomes an enormous distance at the end. Our trouble also lies not in a few wrong actions but in the aim itself being bent. Paul sealed this diagnosis in one sentence: “for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God” (Rom 3:23, ESV). The verb behind “have sinned” (hamartanō) carries that same picture of missing the mark, and the target the arrow misses is called the glory of God. This is the glory we heard about in the first story, where it meant weight. Sin is an arrow that falls short of the glory we were made to carry. It is not the story of a few unusual people. It is everyone’s.

So before sin is doing bad things, it is leaving the true spring that could quench our thirst. Like an arrow that has lost its target, a person who has lost the spring aims at the wrong places. Someone who has left the spring is thirsty, and a thirsty person will dig any hole. The cistern David dug was another man’s wife. The cistern the man with the silver dug was a retreat into being a beast. The cistern each of us digs will differ: the next achievement, someone’s approval, something else again. A good grade moistens the throat for a moment, then dries out before the next exam. Approval fills us for a moment, then leaves us craving more. A broken cistern cannot ease thirst for long, because the water leaks. So we dig deeper and wider, and grow thirstier by exactly as much as we dig. We are like someone who has spent a whole life digging wells and has never once drunk.

This leaving had a beginning. The first book of the Bible records the day the first humans left the spring (Gen 3). God let them eat freely from every tree in the garden, forbidding only one. Then the serpent came near and whispered: “You will not surely die,” and “your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God” (Gen 3:4–5, ESV). They believed that voice instead of the Giver, and moved from the place of receiving to the place of grasping. When they heard God walking in the garden, they hid from his face among the trees, for the first time. The first thing God asked them when he came looking was “Where are you?” (Gen 3:9, ESV). That choice did not end with one person. “Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned” (Rom 5:12, ESV). The cisterns we each dig are the long continuation of the leaving that began that day. The church calls this leaving the fall: something made good, torn loose from its place.

Some of you may be tilting your heads. Why should one person’s choice, long ago, be my story? It is a fair question. The Bible treats the first human as the representative of the whole human family. As a promise made by a nation’s representative binds every citizen, the first human’s leaving became the starting point for all of us. And, frankly, no one taught us, and still we hide in the same way when we have done wrong. But Paul did not end this principle of representation in despair. As through one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, he wrote, so through one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous (see Rom 5:18–19). If we lost through one man, there is a way to be found through one man. Who that one is we will meet in the next story.

This is where the second story has arrived. We came to be as we are not first because of a few bad deeds, but because we left the spring. This is why the awaited guest came to feel like a number, and why a being made so precious weighs itself so light. In the first story we heard that we are guests, set at the table of a house called the universe. The room had been carefully prepared, and the table was laid to overflowing. But the guest left the room. He left the table of the one who made him and went off to dig cisterns of his own. That is how we became numbers. And before this leaving harms me, it is a turning of my back on the owner of the spring. In front of a holy God, this is not lightly covered over. The Bible puts it plainly: “the wages of sin is death” (Rom 6:23, ESV). An arrow that misses its target does not simply drop to the ground and end there.

At this point the king finally broke. What came from his mouth was no excuse. “I have sinned against the LORD” (2 Sam 12:13, ESV). Since sin is first of all a turning of our back on God, David’s first words were turned toward him. The king did not defend himself. Before Nathan’s finger, he bowed his head. A poem he wrote about it later survives (Ps 51), and in it he says, “my sin is ever before me” (Ps 51:3, ESV). A man who had been running turned at last and looked honestly at the cistern he had dug. In the same poem he also asked God to create a clean heart in him and renew a right spirit within him. It was the prayer of someone who knows he cannot fix himself. David knew he could not fill, by his own strength, the cistern he had dug by his own strength. What he asked for was not a better self but a hand that makes new.

After David’s confession, Nathan says one thing more. Where that sentence leads is not yet for us to say. But one thing I will point out now: the collapse was not the end. There is a door, and a person who has honestly seen their own face is finally able to recognize it. How that door opens, the next story will show.

The man in the novel, meanwhile, is still standing at the bishop’s door. Gripped by the collar by a gendarme, he waits for the door of the house he robbed and fled to open. What will the old man who called him brother last night say to him this morning, now that he is a thief? All of us stand together before a door that has not yet opened: David, that man, and we ourselves, who have now seen our own faces in the mirror.

We wait together for the door to open.

My Story — Before You Close the Book

We have come through the second story. Once again three voices circled the same spot. The story of us, who live in prisons we built ourselves, asked the question. The story of a man who lived nineteen years as a number lived it out. The story of David standing before Nathan held up a mirror to our faces. What the three pointed to together was not a light truth. We came to be like this, not first because of a few bad deeds, but because we left the table of the one who made us and dug cisterns of our own. Yet the story did not stop at condemnation. It stopped just before a voice we are readier to hear once we have looked honestly at our own face.

The question we folded away in the first story (why does living in a room this lavishly decorated hurt so often?) got the first piece of an answer here. First, this: my pain is not the price of my own failing. Pain reaches the innocent too. But the Bible does say where it entered this room. The room was not built wrong. It began where the first guest left the spring. Once the whole room had lost its spring, pain stopped choosing and reached everyone. The remaining pieces are still to come.

Before we move on to the next story, pause here over three things. This is not a time to punish yourself. It is a time to look, once, in the mirror at the face you have long put off seeing.

Story Three · Redemption

The Silver Candlesticks

The last story closed with three people standing before one door: David, who saw his own face when the mirror was turned around; a thief dragged in by gendarmes; and the rest of us, watching. The door had not opened, and the question had no answer yet. What would that door hand to a man standing where death was deserved? Now it is about to open.

Opening — Jean Valjean

Imagine the man who ran from the bishop’s house with the silverware. The next morning, at the moment he is dragged back to that door, he might say it like this.

One sentence is all it takes.

I am standing at that door now. The gendarmes have me by the collar. It is the house whose wall I climbed at dawn. Last night an old man in this house called me brother, laid silver spoons at my place, and gave me a bed in the corner. That silver is in a gendarme’s hand now.

The old man has only to open the door and say one sentence: this silver is mine, and that man stole it. Then I will spend the rest of my life in chains. Strangely, I am not afraid of that. That world I know. Steal, get caught, get locked up. I lived there for nineteen years.

What frightens me is something else. When the door opens, last night’s face will look at me. The eyes that called me brother will see a thief. I almost wish those eyes would hate me. Hatred I know how to take. What I do not know is what to do if the eyes never hate me at all.

I hear the latch turn.

Our Story · What Can't Be Repaid

The Person Who Lends Their Name

Anyone who has filled out the paperwork for a first apartment or a first loan knows the blank. The offer has come, the documents are gathered, and one line is still empty: co-signer. It asks for the name of someone who will take the responsibility if you can’t. Whatever your past years looked like, on that page you are a person who has not yet proved anything. Then someone writes in their own name. The landlord and the bank don’t know you yet, but they know this person, and the door opens. The signature means one thing. If this person does not pay, I will.

So a co-signature is not a light thing. To write your name beside someone else’s debt is to promise that if they fall, you will fall in their place. We learn early not to sign such papers carelessly, and we don’t sign them for anyone. We sign for someone we truly love.

Look closely at what a co-signer does and a strange structure appears. The debt does not vanish. Its owner changes. Whatever the receiver is freed from lands, exactly, on the one who gave. The Bible has an unfamiliar title for Jesus: “the guarantor of a better covenant” (Heb 7:22, ESV). A guarantor is someone who writes his own name beside another person’s debt. What that title means, we will see in a moment, in the doorway of a house.

People Who Hate to Owe

There is a small scuffle that happens over a cup of coffee. Two cards come out at the register, and “I’ve got this one” goes back and forth. Being on the receiving end makes us a little uneasy, so as we make the next plan we quietly promise ourselves: next time, it’s on me. Inside that small scuffle sits an old habit of the heart. We find it hard to owe.

If someone buys us dinner, we feel better once we have bought the next one. A wedding gift is answered with a wedding gift, and a holiday present with one of about the same value. So we keep a ledger. It is invisible, but the heart holds a fairly exact record of who gave us what and what we still owe. A relationship feels fair when what goes out matches what comes in, and when the fairness holds, the relationship is easy.

The habit makes us courteous. The trouble starts when something arrives that the ledger cannot hold.

In a life, a day comes when we are given something we have no hope of repaying. An older friend who handed us a large sum, no strings attached, in our hardest season. A senior colleague who covered our mistake under their own name. A friend who stayed beside us in silence for months. Something unexpected happens in the heart. Gratitude grows, and past a certain point gratitude turns into weight. And weight makes us avoid the person.

The bigger the kindness, the harder the meeting. We keep meaning to call and keep putting it off. Every week of delay makes the call harder, until a back on the street that only resembles theirs makes us slow our steps. The person who was best to us is the one we avoid most diligently. We don’t resent them. When we stand in front of them, the size of what we haven’t repaid stands there too. The benefactor’s face becomes a mirror that shows our thanks and our empty hands together. Before a debt with no way of repayment, the heart picks avoidance over gratitude. That isn’t ingratitude. The grace is simply bigger than we can stand in front of. Sometimes a person runs from a love he cannot repay. The man at the table with the silver spoons, in the second story, did exactly that. As warmth reaching a long-frozen hand comes first as pain, so for a man who has forgotten how to receive, a gift is a threat. We will meet the man who ran from love in the night again soon.

A scholar has looked closely at why we are so clumsy at receiving. Lewis Hyde, an American literary scholar, gave a whole book to the question, The Gift. He had been studying works of art and the gift customs of old tribal societies, and he came to this conclusion: there are two different economies in the world.

One is the economy of the market. It is where you pay a price and get a thing, where you give as much as you get and repay as much as you receive. Everything here has a price, and when the deal is done, the relationship is done. When the accounts balance, no one owes anyone anything, and with nothing owed, nothing ties us together. Fair, and cold.

The other is the economy of the gift. Here nothing is priced. A mother nursing her baby writes no invoice, and a friend who offers comfort sends no bill. What interested Hyde was the strange law of this world: a gift dies the moment its price is paid. Once you start counting the cost in the world of gifts, the gift has already stopped being a gift. Something paid for is a trade. And a trade ends a relationship, while a gift opens one, because what stays unpaid keeps two people bound. Where the accounts are settled, people become strangers. Only where they are unsettled do people keep meeting. Perhaps our rush to repay is a way of staying untangled. A relationship with no debts, seen from the other side, is a relationship that can end at any time.

The trouble is that we have grown so fluent in the language of the market that we have almost forgotten the language of the gift. We translate everything into a trade. When we are loved, we calculate what to repay. When we are shown kindness, we wonder about the motive. We learned that nothing is free, so in front of what looks free we go looking for the hidden bill. Someone who suspects every price will not recognize a love that has none.

Ten Million Dollars and a Hundred

Suppose there is a man who carried a debt he could never pay in a lifetime. Say ten million dollars. One day someone paid all of it for him. When he heard that the debt was gone, he thanked his benefactor and took a hundred dollars out of his wallet to hold out. He wanted to chip in a little.

Is this moving, or does something feel off? The hundred dollars is certainly sincere. But to offer a hundred to the one who paid ten million says less about sincerity than about how little he has grasped the gift. It is closer to rudeness than to humility. If he understood what ten million means, there would be one thing to do: take it with both hands, bow his head, and say thank you. Any gesture of adding something pulls the gift down into a trade.

Here two words part ways: a paycheck and a gift. A paycheck is pay for work done. I made the claim to it myself, so on payday we do not bow our heads. A gift is different. It is the giver’s heart, not anything in me, that makes me fit to receive it, so before a gift the head bows. Yet many people try to receive God’s love as a paycheck. If I live well, if I pray hard, I will have earned that much. They try to repay the love with hard work, as if filling out a list of qualifications. But if that love was a gift from the beginning and never a paycheck, all this arithmetic points the wrong way. Effort to build a claim is an attempt to turn a gift into wages, and it is no different from holding out a hundred dollars to the one who paid ten million.

Two lives are possible after the same gift. One person lives under a sense of debt, always thinking about what is left to pay, and the weight is heavy. The other lives in enjoyment. Knowing there is no repaying, he is lighter, and he is grateful. One becomes a debtor, and the other becomes a guest at the feast. The difference is not the size of the gift. It is whether it was received as wages or as a gift.

Only When We Become Parents

Those of us who grew up without learning how to receive sometimes learn it in an unexpected place. We become parents.

Some of you will remember the night you first held a newborn. The baby can do nothing. No thank-you, no eye contact, not even a break in the crying. The parent walks the floor with the child all night. And on that night something dawns. I don’t expect this child to pay me back. I don’t even hope the child will remember these nights. Giving without calculation, without conditions, with no repayment assumed: such a thing does exist. It is the moment when someone who has kept a ledger all his life finds a love with no ledger pouring out of himself for the first time. If the child grows up and offers to repay those nights, the parent will laugh. It was not given to be repaid. The best thanks a child can give is growing well, eating that love freely and getting tall on it.

In that moment one more thing follows. I was held like this too. On countless nights I cannot remember, someone paced the floor with me. It is a love I have no memory of receiving and am certain I received. All of us began our lives on that kind of love. That we know even a little of what it is to give without wanting anything back is because we first received it that way. How far back this heart reaches, we will ask in a little while.

One more thing belongs on the list of what cannot be repaid: forgiveness. The philosopher Hannah Arendt observed that human life contains deeds that cannot be undone. A wrong done to me cannot be erased by turning back time. What has been done has been done. The one way out of that irreversibility, Arendt said, is forgiveness. Forgiveness does not make the past as if it never happened. It breaks the chain so that the past stops binding the future. And this can never be done by trade. We all know a relationship in which an apology came from the heart and forgiveness never did. Apology and restitution are things I can do. Forgiveness comes only when it flows freely from the other person’s heart. It is not bought but received, and so the forgiven always stand empty-handed. That empty hand is nothing to be ashamed of. It is the proof that the forgiveness was real.

That is as far as we can feel our way into what cannot be repaid. A gift dies the moment its price is paid. For some gifts, receiving is itself a way of honoring the giver. Forgiveness cannot be bought. Each of these insights is right. But there is one thing they cannot tell us: whether such a gift exists. Has a gift too large to repay, a forgiveness given with no qualification, ever arrived in this world as more than an idea? Philosophy can go as far as saying that such a gift ought to exist. It cannot say that it came. Between “ought to exist” and “does exist” there is a gap that argument cannot close. Only an event can.

We have such a story. It is about a man who received love and ran, and the love that waited for him. It happens at the door where we stopped. The gendarmes had a man by the collar in front of the house he robbed, and the door was about to open.

One Man's Story · Why Did You Leave the Candlesticks?

Before the door opens, let us step into that house’s morning.

That morning Madame Magloire went to the cupboard, as she did every day, to take out the silver, and she froze. The cupboard was empty. Six place settings and the large silver ladle had vanished in the night. She searched the house. She searched the kitchen, then the dining room, and last she went out to the garden, where she found a trampled flower bed beneath the wall. The marks of someone who had climbed over and run. Her face went white. It was him. Last night’s drifter. The man they had taken in without even asking his name had gone over the wall with the best of what the house owned. She had counted that silver every day as she polished it. Her long years were worked into it. Why hadn’t they bolted the door, she muttered at the empty cupboard, again and again. Hadn’t she said so last night too?

When she ran out shouting, the bishop was bent over a bed in the garden, studying a broken plant beside the empty basket someone had tossed away in the night. The silver was gone, she said, it had been stolen, it was certainly that man’s doing. She poured it all out without taking a breath. The bishop heard every word, his eyes never leaving the plant. Then he straightened up and asked quietly.

“Madame, was that silver ever ours?”

She had no answer. Brushing the soil from his hands, he went on. That silver had belonged to the poor for a long time, he said. We had only been keeping it. And the man who came to this house last night was, by any measure, a poor man.

At breakfast that morning there were wooden spoons on the table. Madame Magloire set hers down with a clatter. On the mantel, the two silver candlesticks stood where they had always stood. Perhaps the thief, searching the cupboard in the dark, had not seen them. Perhaps he could not bring himself to reach that far. Either way, the candlesticks had stayed.

Then there was a knock at the door.

Three gendarmes stood outside. Between them, gripped by the collar, a man stood with his head down. He was the one who had eaten soup with a silver spoon at this table last night, and the one who had gone over this wall at dawn. In the gendarmes’ hands was the silver that had spilled from his knapsack. He had told them that an old priest gave it to him, and they had come to this door to check the story. He could not lift his head. One sentence would settle it now. This silver is mine, and that man stole it. With that, the story would collapse and everything would be over. He knew it. A life sentence at the galleys. He knew better than anyone the price the world sets on a convict’s second offense. Strangely, some corner of him felt almost at ease. This was the world he knew. Steal, get caught, get locked up. The world that had wavered for a moment last night had simply settled back into place, he thought, with his head down.

The bishop came to the doorway. The moment he saw the man they were holding, he said:

“Ah, there you are! I am delighted to see you.”

The man looked up. He did not understand. The bishop turned to that face and went on.

“But why did you leave the candlesticks? I gave you those along with the silver. They are silver too, and worth two hundred francs by themselves. Why did you take only the plates?”

His eyes wavered. The gendarmes glanced at each other. The one who seemed to be in charge asked, hesitating, whether the bishop meant that he had given this silver to the man. Was the man’s story true after all? The bishop said yes. The gendarme looked from the papers to the man to the bishop and back, then asked whether in that case they might let the man go. Of course, said the bishop.

The gendarmes let go of his collar. He stood nailed to the spot, with the face of someone who does not know what has happened to him, dazed, like a sleepwalker.

The bishop went inside and came out with the two silver candlesticks from the mantel. He pressed them into the man’s hands. They were the same candlesticks that had lit last night’s table, both lit even for a supper of plain soup. The weight of the solid silver settled in his palms. His hands shook. It was a weight he had not known when he stole. In the dark, sweeping silver into his knapsack, it had been money and nothing more. Received in the light, with both hands, it was something else. Madame Magloire gripped the doorpost and watched. Silver she had counted for a lifetime was crossing into a stranger’s hands, and she watched it in silence to the end. The gendarmes watched too. Only then did the bishop tell them they could go. They left.

The bishop leaned toward him. In a voice low enough that no one else could hear, he said:

“Do not forget what you promised me: that you would use this silver to become an honest man.”

He had promised nothing. Not last night, not this morning had he promised that bishop a thing. Yet the bishop spoke like someone reminding him of a promise already given, as if he were planting it in him right then.

And then, looking at the man with the candlesticks in his hands, the bishop spoke gravely.

“Jean Valjean, my brother, you no longer belong to evil but to good. I have bought your soul. I withdraw it from the dark and give it to God.”

Jean Valjean. For the first time in nineteen years, that name was spoken not with contempt but with the word brother. The prison had taken his name and given him a number. This bishop erased the number and gave the name back. He gave it to the man who had robbed him, and he gave it with the word brother.

Valjean could not say a word. No greeting, no thanks, no excuse. With two candlesticks in his arms, he left that house. He walked like a man running, but this time he could not have said what he was running from.

His Story · The Great Exchange

The Father Who Ran

As that figure walks away with the candlesticks, we have to ask. What happened at that doorway? How is it possible that a thief receives a gift, and that blessing arrives where death was deserved? To answer, there is a story Jesus told, the most famous parable in the Bible, the one we call the parable of the prodigal son.

A father had two sons. One day the younger came to him and said, Father, give me my share of the estate now. By the custom of the day, an inheritance came after the father died. The request amounts to saying: I can’t wait for you to die, so treat me as if you were dead already. It means that he wants the father’s property but not the father. Astonishingly, the father grants this cruel request. He divides his property, and he watches his son gather it up and leave for a far country.

What happened in the far country is what we would guess. While the money lasted, there were friends and there were parties. When it ran out, a famine came, the friends disappeared, and the son became a hired hand feeding pigs in a foreign land. To a Jew, pigs are unclean animals. A Jewish son, in a Gentile country, had fallen so far that he longed to fill his belly with the pods the pigs ate, and no one gave him any (see Luke 15:16). The friends he bought left with the money. In his father’s house even the hired hands had enough to eat, and the freedom he bought by leaving his father ended in a hunger worse than the pigs’. The picture from the second story is here again. A person who leaves home does not find freedom. He finds hunger. Someone who leaves the spring always ends up beside a broken cistern.

Beside the pigs, the son comes to his senses. He decides to go home. But listen to how he plans to go home. As he walks, he prepares a speech. He will say that he has sinned against heaven and against his father, that he no longer deserves to be called a son, and that his father might take him on as one of his hired hands.

Take me on as a hired hand. In that last sentence is his arithmetic. He has lost the standing of a son, so now he will work it off. He will earn his keep. What he is carrying is a job application. Unable to take the gift as a gift, he will take it as wages, the familiar arithmetic we met earlier. The hand that held out a hundred dollars before ten million is here holding a hired man’s application.

But the heart of this parable comes next. The Bible says:

“And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him” (Luke 15:20, ESV).

While he was still a long way off, the father saw him. It was no accident. Only someone who has watched the end of that road every day can pick out a single speck on a far horizon. The father had been waiting since the day his son left. The parable does not say how long he kept looking down that road each day. It does not have to. If the waiting had lasted a day or two, no eye would be sharp enough to pick one speck out of the distance. And the father runs. In that world, for an elderly head of a household to run was to throw away his dignity. Hiking up his long robe, bare-legged, an old man ran down a road the whole village could see. When the son came into the village, pointing fingers would greet him. The father ran out first and stood in the way with his own body.

Then the son’s speech begins. Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. And there it breaks off. The last sentence, take me on as a hired hand, never makes it out of his mouth. The father cuts in and calls to the servants. Bring the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his hand and sandals on his feet. Kill the fattened calf (see Luke 15:22–23). A robe, a ring, and sandals do not belong to a hired hand. They belong to a son. The father never let the speech reach its last line. He put the son’s standing back on him before any contract could be offered.

We have seen this at the doorway where candlesticks were added to a thief’s armload, on the morning when a thief was handed what he could never repay and was given back the name of a brother. Myriel’s doorway was this parable’s courtyard, and the silver candlesticks were the best robe.

Here we can return to a question we set aside earlier. It was about the night spent holding a newborn, the love that gives without wanting repayment, which we saw pouring out of ourselves for the first time. How far back does that heart reach? The answer is inside this parable. That heart did not begin with us. It came from the running father. There is a sense in which the love without calculation that we hold for our children is a faint copy of the love God first held for us. The original is in heaven.

What That Sentence Is Called

Here we pay a debt left over from the second story. When David collapsed before Nathan and said, “I have sinned against the LORD,” I mentioned that Nathan said one thing more. I set it aside then, because it was not yet time to say it. This is that sentence.

Nathan said to David, “The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die” (2 Sam 12:13, ESV).

You shall not die. David had sentenced himself to death with his own mouth. Told a story he took to be about someone else, he had ruled that a man who did such a thing deserved to die. The moment the verdict turned toward him, the king was a dead man. And in that place the opposite sentence falls. You will not die.

We heard that sentence at the doorway where a thief stood held by gendarmes, from a man who could have sent him to the galleys for life with one word. This silver was my gift. In saying so, the bishop was telling him, you will not die. Of course, the illustration has limits. The bishop took a loss but did not take the thief’s punishment in his place, and what he told the gendarmes was not true. Still, the scene shows the outline of the original: release arriving where death was deserved. The Bible calls this the forgiveness of sin, or pardon. That was exactly what Nathan’s sentence was.

Yet this sentence comforts us and troubles us at once. David’s sin was not made to never have happened. A man died, and a household broke. Set what began on that rooftop beside the Ten Commandments and the weight shows. It begins with the tenth, do not covet your neighbor’s wife, and runs to the seventh, do not commit adultery, then the eighth, do not steal, the taking of another man’s lamb, which Nathan’s parable exposed, then the ninth, the lie meant to cover it all, and last the sixth, do not murder. Half the tablets broke on one rooftop. Beneath all of them lies the collapse of the first commandment: he set his own desire on the throne in the place of God. A king had tried to stand above the King. Where did the price go? When a judge sets a guilty person free at no cost, we do not call it mercy. We call it injustice. If forgiveness swallowed justice, it would be not holy but careless. So we cannot help asking. Who paid for that sentence, you shall not die?

The same question stands at Myriel’s door. The thief was released without paying. But the price did not vanish. The bishop paid for the silver, from his own property, at his own loss. The release was not free. It was free only to the one who received it. To the one who gave, it cost all he had. What the bishop carried was a loss, not a punishment. Even so, the scene shows the outline of the co-signer we spoke of earlier. The debt does not disappear. Its owner changes. Behind everything received for nothing stands someone who paid in full. What we call a gift is not a thing with no price. It is a thing whose price we did not pay.

In fact, Nathan’s pardon had grounds. This prophet had stood before the same king some years earlier, and what he brought then was not a rebuke but a promise. In God’s covenant with David’s house, the Davidic covenant, a promise God made on his own initiative, there is this clause.

“I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son. When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him with the rod of men, with the stripes of the sons of men, but my steadfast love will not depart from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I put away from before you” (2 Sam 7:14–15, ESV).

If he sins, there will be the rod, but God’s steadfast love will not be taken away. A rod, but no abandonment. This clause was spoken first about the son who would follow David, yet the promise of that favor held David’s whole house. David’s not dying was inside that favor. That does not mean his sin passed lightly. In the same account, Nathan told him that the sword would never leave his house, and the child born of that night did not live (see 2 Sam 12:10–18). The discipline came. But one thing never came: the verdict that a man who did such a thing deserved to die, the death sentence David pronounced with his own mouth. He was disciplined, and death passed him by. Where did that death go? That is the question this story still holds.

Swapped Robes

With that question, we go to a hill outside the gates of Jerusalem called Golgotha.

A thousand years after David, one who came as David’s descendant hung on a cross on that hill. A Roman cross was a criminal’s frame. But the one hanging on it had no crime. The governor who tried him said three times that he found no guilt in him. A man without sin died in a prisoner’s place. Seven hundred years earlier, the Bible had written down what this execution meant, in the pen of the prophet Isaiah.

“But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned—every one—to his own way; and the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isa 53:5–6, ESV).

He and our. These two words trade places in line after line. He was pierced, and the transgressions are ours. He was chastised, and the peace is ours. A few lines earlier, the song says that people thought he was stricken, smitten by God (see Isa 53:4). A few lines later, it says he was cut off from the land of the living, stricken for the transgression of the people (see Isa 53:8). The death that passed David by fell on the sinless son of David. Paul explains that God was patient and passed over former sins so that at the cross he might show his righteousness (see Rom 3:25–26). Nathan’s pardon was not an empty place where a price should have been. It was a place where the one to pay had already been named. We went each to his own way, we who left the spring to look for cisterns, and he bore the iniquity. The places were swapped. The apostle Paul compressed the exchange into one sentence.

“For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Cor 5:21, ESV).

The one who knew no sin becomes sin, and we sinners become righteousness: the standing of being counted right before God. God’s holy anger at evil, and at us who are caught in it (more on this later), he did not wave away. He sent his Son, and the Son received that wrath in our place. His spotless robe is put on me, and my filthy robe is put on him. My being made right and his standing in the sinner’s place are not two separate events. They are one exchange, one set of clothes passing back and forth. It is the most unfair trade in history, one side giving everything and the other receiving everything. People have called it the great exchange. Nathan’s sentence left the payment unnamed. At Golgotha it is made. David did not die, not because there was no price but because the one to pay it had already been appointed. That is why the Bible calls Jesus “the guarantor of a better covenant” (Heb 7:22, ESV). A thousand years earlier, his name was already written beside David’s debt. Whoever could sign that line needed three qualifications. Because it was a human debt, he had to be human. Because it was owed to God, he had to be God. And he needed to have no debt of his own to pay. Only the one who is true God and true man and knew no sin could write a name there. No one else could.

The last words he spoke from the cross matter for that reason.

“When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, ‘It is finished,’ and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit” (John 19:30, ESV).

It is finished. The word behind it is tetelestai, which means that a task has been carried through to its end. In the same chapter John uses it twice: Jesus, knowing that all was now finished (see John 19:28), and then, It is finished. The Greek word is also said to have been written on receipts in that era to mark a debt as paid in full. So the last cry from the cross was a declaration. It declared that the price piled up in the ledger of everyone who comes to him had now been paid. These were the words of a victor. The death did not end because things went wrong. It ended because the plan was complete. Nothing more remains to pay.

You will remember what Myriel said to Valjean. I have bought your soul. The price at that door was silver and candlesticks. At Golgotha the price was the Son of God. If the bishop’s silver bought the rest of one man’s life, the blood of Christ bought our eternity. The silver candlesticks are a beautiful story, but before the original they point to, they are only a shadow.

There is one more thing to notice in this exchange. It is not an equal trade. If he had taken away only the sin, we would be back at zero. What happened was more. In the place stripped bare, righteousness was put on. Myriel, too, did not stop at covering the theft. He added the candlesticks. Pardon came, and a gift on top of it. Cancellation came, and an inheritance on top of that. Paul wrote the grammar of this surplus in a single line.

“…where sin increased, grace abounded all the more” (Rom 5:20, ESV).

Abounded means overflowed. It does not mean “made up for.” What was lost was not barely patched. Grace was poured out larger than the loss. The morning at the doorway, when two candlesticks were laid on top in the hands of the man who stole the silver, was a distant echo of this exchange.

A Person Whose Account Is Settled

If the price has been paid, one question is left. Whose does that paid price become?

Paul’s answer goes like this.

“For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast” (Eph 2:8–9, ESV).

He says gift. He drives in the nail: not a paycheck, not a result of works. What qualifies us to receive is the Giver’s heart, not anything we did. So what we can do is not hold out a hired man’s contract but receive with both hands. Before a gift we cannot repay, what we need is a way to receive. The way a person learns on becoming a parent becomes, here, the language of Scripture.

In fact, the Bible once set these two words, wages and gift, side by side in a single sentence.

“For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Rom 6:23, ESV).

Wages are paid in exact proportion to work done. The pay that sin issues, the sentence says, is death. It never runs late and is never docked, the most honest accounting in the world. And in the middle of the sentence the accounting ends and the economy of the gift begins. Free gift means given without price. Our talk of paycheck and gift, it turns out, was a long commentary on this one verse. Death is earned and received. Eternal life is received and enjoyed. Behind that death, the Bible sees something it calls the wrath of God. It is not a mood swing. A love that feels nothing in the face of evil that wrecks what it loves is not love. The wrath is the holy, righteous resistance of love against evil and against us who stand in it. And Paul describes the people who have been rescued from that wrath: “Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God” (Rom 5:9, ESV). To be justified is to be declared right before God.

Right here, though, many people stumble. We receive it by faith, we are told, and now the faith is the problem. My faith is so small, and it wavers so often. Can something like that receive anything?

Imagine a bridge over a deep canyon. One person strides across. Another crawls across with trembling knees. Who is safer? If the bridge is steel, both are equally safe. If the bridge is rotten wood, even the boldest person falls. What carries us across is not the state of the knees but the strength of what the feet rest on. We live this truth every day. No one checks how much they trust a chair before sitting down. They sit. What holds them up is the legs of the chair, not their confidence. Faith is like that. Its strength is not in faith itself but in what faith rests on. So faith is not merit. It is open hands. Hands held out to receive a gift do not earn the gift’s price. A trembling empty hand and a bold empty hand receive the same gift. But open hands also mean something else: letting go of what we were gripping. The Bible calls the turning that comes with faith repentance, a turning around. A hand still holding the shovel that dug cisterns cannot be empty. It means setting down what we clutched to prove ourselves, and the direction of life that said we could live without God. Repentance is not a payment for earning forgiveness. It is turning around toward a Father who is already running to meet you.

What is placed in those open hands, John wrote down like this.

“But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God” (John 1:12, ESV).

Receive means to welcome. Those who believed in his name: here again the Bible speaks in the language of names. Not the one who passed an exam, not the one who proved a qualification, but the one who believed a name and held out empty hands. What is given is the standing of a child. We have followed the story of a man who lived by a number getting his name back, and here the Bible goes one step further. The person who believes in his name receives a new family name in front of his own. The family name is child of God.

How deep this goes is written at the head of that same chapter, right before the sentence about the gift.

“And you were dead in the trespasses and sins” (Eph 2:1, ESV). A few lines on, Paul says that God, even when we were dead in our trespasses, “made us alive together with Christ” (Eph 2:5, ESV).

Dead. That is the vase from the second story. The flower cut from its root, lovely for a moment, already dying. The Bible does not describe salvation as a sick person getting better. It says a dead person was made alive. It is not treatment. It is new life. What no gardener can do, bring a cut flower back to life, was made possible on the cross, and the Holy Spirit makes the dead alive, enables them to believe, and joins them to that life. Not only was the price paid. Life was joined again.

And to the one who has received, a verdict is given.

“There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus” (Rom 8:1, ESV).

No condemnation: it is the language of a courtroom. When a judge pronounces not guilty, from that moment the defendant is legally a free person. Even if the heart is still heavy, even if guilt lingers, the force of the verdict does not depend on feelings. Salvation is like that. It rests not on my mood but on a verdict. The ground of the verdict was paid at Golgotha two thousand years ago, and the verdict is declared to everyone who comes to him. Some days faith runs hot and some days cold, but the verdict is not erased by my body temperature. Some people, acquitted, go on living hunched over like prisoners for a while. Long imprisonment gets into the body. But hunched shoulders do not void the verdict. What is needed is not another trial. It is to trust the verdict already given and straighten the shoulders.

This is how the story of the person digging cisterns ends. A thirsty person did not go and find the spring. The spring came looking for the thirsty. We were found with the shovel still in our hands and dirt on our palms, and from behind us the living water spoke first. Like the father who ran while the son was still far off, like the house that was never locked before anyone knocked, the price was paid before we asked.

Even these open hands are not something we squeeze out ourselves. The One who brings that price home to a person’s heart, the Bible calls the Holy Spirit. To a scholar who came to him by night, Jesus said that unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God (see John 3:5), and then added, “The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit” (John 3:8, ESV). If something in you stirs in front of this story, it may be the sound of that wind. This door always opens first from the other side, not from ours.

That spring has a name. On the last day of the great feast in Jerusalem, when the whole city was stirring with the ritual of drawing water to pour out at the altar, Jesus stood up in the middle of it and cried out.

“If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink” (John 7:37, ESV).

To me. The spring gave its address. He does not tell the hand that dug cisterns to examine its shovel first. He does not scold the thirst. He says only, come. And anyone: it is an invitation with no threshold of qualification.

The prophet Isaiah left one more sentence about what kind of love this is.

“Behold, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands; your walls are continually before me” (Isa 49:16, ESV).

Engraved on the palms. What is written on paper can be lost, and what is written on the heart can blur. What is engraved on the palm does not fade while the one who engraved it lives. It is the place you see every time you open your hand, the place that moves with everything you do. First, this word is God’s promise not to forget his people in Zion, Jerusalem. Christians, reading it, have remembered the nail marks in the hands of the risen Lord, which he showed his disciples (see John 20:27). The risen body did not erase the scars. They stayed, not as wounds to hide but as the marks of his love.

If the cross had been the end of the story, tetelestai would have remained one great man’s last words. But three days later, at dawn, the tomb was empty. That the one who declared it finished is alive now is why the declaration has not lost its force in two thousand years. The resurrection is not decoration in this story. It is a pillar. When Paul lists those who met the risen Jesus, he writes that at one time more than five hundred saw him, and that most of them were still alive (see 1 Cor 15:5–8). It was a list of witnesses the readers of the letter could go and question. It is also why frightened disciples who had scattered when Jesus was arrested became, a few weeks later, people who staked their lives on telling this news. No one gives up his life for a story he made up.

This love began with full knowledge of every failure we have yet to commit. It knew every stumble, past and still to come, and chose to pay anyway.

Return to a declaration Jesus made about himself. “I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved” (John 10:9, ESV). Myriel’s door is beautiful. But that door, too, was finally a shadow, as the silver candlesticks were a shadow of Golgotha. No door in this world can take a person all the way in. So Jesus does not say, I will show you a good door. He says, I am the door. He did not stop at being the one who opens the door. He became the door itself. A door where everyone who enters is saved, a door that is not locked at night, and, as the last story will show, a door no one can shut. The door we admired in the streets of Digne was pointing all along at this original.

A Prayer in Answer to the Invitation

The price has been paid. The gift belongs to anyone who holds out empty hands. If this story has made you want to set down the shovel beside the cistern you have dug for so long, there is room to say a few words right where you are. You don’t need a long prayer. The door was never locked. Your knees may tremble, and the bridge will not give way. What carries you across is the bridge, not your certainty.

God,
I have come here thirsty.
I have lived away from you, digging my own cisterns with my own strength.
I confess that this was sin against you.
Please forgive me.
I believe that Jesus died on the cross in my place
and paid the full price of my sin.
I believe that he rose again on the third day.
I receive this gift with empty hands.
Now I turn around and welcome Jesus as the Lord of my life.
I am not my own now. I am yours.
In Jesus’ name I pray. Amen.

If you have prayed this prayer, what you hold on to is Christ and his promise, not the size of your prayer. “All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out” (John 6:37, ESV). I hope you will let a Christian friend or a nearby church know. The church confirms this confession together in baptism and rejoices over it. It is a great gift to have someone near who will rejoice with you over a first confession.

This door is not locked tomorrow, or the day after.

The last declaration of this story belongs to anyone who has received this gift with empty hands. In Christ, you are a person whose price has been paid. The gift that was given not to be repaid but to be enjoyed is yours. You can take your hand out of the pocket that was fishing for a hundred dollars to set beside ten million. It is finished. That word, proclaimed at Golgotha, has been proclaimed today over your name too. And the door is open. It was open before you knocked.

My Story — Before You Close the Book

We have come through the third story. It had a different grain from the first two. If the first story showed how precious we are, and the second how deeply we are lost, the third was about the price paid to bring the lost one home. Before a gift we cannot repay, what we learn is not how to repay but how to receive, and the price of that gift has already been paid by Another. The question we folded away in the first story received its second piece as well. The pain we began with was not ignored. The price of our sin was paid by the one who built the room, and he even carried our griefs and sorrows in his own body (see Isa 53:4). Now pause and bring this story into your own place.

Our Story. Where in my life do I feel the pull to repay, to earn a place in front of love that was given freely?

One Man’s Story. Valjean received a grace he couldn’t repay, and he ran. He didn’t know where he was going, only that he couldn’t stand in front of that love. Has there been a moment when love came to me as a burden instead of a comfort? If nothing comes to mind, it’s enough to ask which is easier for me, receiving or giving.

Story Four · New Life

The Name Restored

The last story closed with a declaration. You are someone whose price has been paid, and the words It is finished have been spoken over your name too. Then the story stopped on a man with two candlesticks in his arms, running from something he could not name. Where did that flight end? Eight years later, who is he? This time we will hear the answer under an unfamiliar name. Even if you haven’t yet taken that declaration as your own, this story won’t feel strange. Nearly everyone has had the morning after a firm resolution, when the same old self shows up again.

Opening — Madeleine

Eight years later, the man living under another name could have said this on a sleepless night. I imagine it so.

People call me Mayor Madeleine.

In this town it is a good name. It built a factory that feeds hundreds of people. It added beds to the hospital. Children who meet it in the street take off their caps. A respectable gentleman stands in front of my mirror. Once, a face in a puddle looked so strange to me that I asked whether it was mine. Tonight I ask the opposite. This face that everyone knows so well: is it truly me?

I made up the name Madeleine. That morning, leaving Digne with two candlesticks in my arms, I buried Jean Valjean. A name with a number on it, a name that a yellow paper followed everywhere, could save no one. Under the new name I lived like a new man. The silver candlesticks still stand on my mantel. A promise to become an honest man, one I never spoke aloud and yet already owe, is what they remind me of every night. And every night the same question rises. Can there be such a thing, anywhere, as an honest man living under an invented name?

Inspector Javert came by this afternoon. He told me Jean Valjean has been caught. A man named Champmathieu will be sentenced tomorrow in court as Jean Valjean. When tomorrow is over, the name Jean Valjean will belong to someone else for good, and I can live as Madeleine until the day I die. No one is hurt. Or rather, when I think of the workers who depend on this factory, perhaps that is the way that saves everyone.

I wanted to stay Madeleine. Yet under this good name I can’t sleep tonight. Now that I think of it, somewhere along the way I stopped growing inside it. Even the finest pot stops a tree once the roots touch its wall. Maybe a person can never rest inside someone else’s name, however fine that name may be.

Our Story · The Face Behind the Mask

The Name on the Desk

On the first day after a promotion, a box of business cards sits on the desk. A hundred pieces of paper, each printed exactly alike, with a name and a new title. The person who opens the box takes one out and studies it for a long while. The paper is crisp and the lettering is clean. Somehow it looks like someone else’s. While the congratulations pour in, a small voice in the back of the mind won’t stop. Maybe people have this wrong. Is the person this title calls for the person sitting in this chair?

A young doctor who has hung her name on an exam-room door for the first time hears the word “Doctor” and realizes, a beat late, that it is her they mean. New parents carrying a baby out of the hospital feel the same. Who decided I was qualified to take charge of this child for a lifetime? I don’t remember passing any review. The name grows first, and the person follows behind.

So it is with the self on our screens, assembled from the best photo and the most impressive news. The better the edited version is received, the shabbier the original seems. On the night in the first story, standing before the mirror, we asked: how much was I worth today? The question on a night years later has shifted a little. Who was I today?

We assumed time would help. We thought that as the years piled up, the awkward distance between the name and the self would close. In many cases the opposite happens. In the first story, a handful of documents could not hold a whole person. As time passes the problem flips, and the documents grow larger than the person. The one printed on the card becomes more admirable than the one in the flesh. From then on, life becomes a play, in which the real person must perform the admirable person on the paper for a lifetime.

Any Day Now, They’ll Find Out

This feeling got its name in 1978. The American psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes kept hearing the same confession in the counseling room. People with clear degrees and credentials said, one after another: I’m not that person. I only got lucky. And someday it will all come out.

The two women named it. In other people’s eyes, a qualified person; in one’s own eyes, a fraud. The world would later call it impostor syndrome. It was first described in accomplished women, but later surveys suggested that it turned up in men and women and in every profession. It also seems to visit people at the top of their fields more often.

There is a paradox buried in this feeling. Common sense says achievement should breed confidence. But in this mind’s bookkeeping, success never gets deposited into my account. What goes well is booked to luck. Only what goes wrong is recorded in my name. So however much I earn, the balance stays empty, and every prize only adds to the height I could fall from. Rereading an acceptance letter again and again, in case it was a mistake, is the same bookkeeping.

The ledger is relentless. Clance even charted the cycle this feeling runs through. A task arrives, and anxiety comes with it. The anxious person either prepares many times harder than anyone else, or freezes and puts it off. Then, when it goes well, the accounting runs like this. The one who over-prepared decides it wasn’t ability, only strain to cover the gaps. The one who put it off and pulled it through decides it was luck again. Whichever way the circle turns, success is never filed as proof of skill, and at the next task the cycle starts over from the beginning.

The weight doesn’t stop there. This mask can’t simply be taken off, because there would be too much to lose. So at some point a person begins to live not for the self but for the reputation. Praise arrives and is set aside: that’s meant for the admirable paper person. Love arrives and doesn’t sink in: they would leave if they saw the real me. Applause always belongs to someone else.

The Courage to Be Imperfect

Psychology didn’t stop at diagnosis. It held out a prescription too. The best known came from the school of the Austrian psychiatrist Alfred Adler. It is called the courage to be imperfect.

One of Adler’s more surprising insights is that feeling inferior is not itself an illness. Feeling lacking is the human default, and that very feeling is what drives a person to grow. The sickness starts when we hide it. Adler saw that the harder someone works to look superior, the deeper the sense of inferiority hiding underneath. The higher the stage is built, the darker it is beneath it.

So to Adlerian eyes, the root of the sickness was clear. It is the desperate effort to seem better than one is. The moment we build a character for the stage to hide our imperfection, we begin to live two lives. One performs the character on stage. The other trembles backstage, afraid of being found out. The answer, then, is to stop performing. To stand among people as the clumsy, lacking person we are, and to accept being disliked for it if that is the price. Adler’s heirs gave this a name: the courage to be imperfect.

The direction is exactly right. This school deserves credit for seeing that the answer is not a better performance but stepping off the stage. Plenty of people have felt their breathing ease at these words. I think of the people who have confessed that a single sentence, you don’t have to be perfect, loosened shoulders that had been tight for ten years.

Arms That Will Receive the Bare Face

But this prescription has a blank on its last line.

Have the courage to take it off, the prescription says. Good. And after that? Courage can carry a person as far as opening the door. It cannot write a guarantee that the face revealed will be welcomed. Accept being disliked: read the other way around, that means there is a real chance of being disliked.

One more question remains, and it is the heavier one. What if what comes out when we take the mask off is more than clumsiness? Adler called what lies at the bottom of us imperfection. Clumsy, lacking, prone to mistakes. If that were all, courage might do. But having come through the first two stories, we have seen something else at that bottom: the hands that dug cracked cisterns, the eyes that coveted what was not theirs, the night a man carried silver out of a house that had fed him. “I’m a little clumsy” and “I am that person” are confessions of different weight. A confession of clumsiness can be made with courage. A confession of sin can’t be made with courage alone. Without the assurance that there are arms that will not throw me away after I say it, no one can say it out loud.

Children know this best. A child who has done wrong hides first. Yet the place the child finally runs to and clings to is the very arms that will scold. A child can name the wrong, though sure to be scolded, because the child knows that even in the scolding those arms won’t let go. Confession is the child of trust before it is the child of courage. Only when arms that will receive come into view does a person open their mouth. People say that what we truly fear is not failure but being found out. Someone who has failed can try again, but someone who has been found out becomes someone who never qualified in the first place. Before that fear, what we need is not a hand pushing at our back but arms waiting on the other side of the door.

None of us has ever named ourselves. The name I use now was fastened to me around the time I was born by someone, usually the very people who had been waiting for me, who chose it slowly and carefully. You may remember the couple in the first story who wrote name candidates on the refrigerator door and tried one aloud each time they walked past. That is how names come. A name is not issued as payment for achievement. It is conferred as a sign of welcome. If so, the sickness we suffer as adults may be a sickness of reversed order: the sickness of trying to earn what should be received, of manufacturing with our own hands what should be bestowed.

I’d like to describe this sickness another way. Anyone who has moved house, changed schools, changed jobs, or married knows the feeling, and so does anyone who has moved to another country. The body needs a day to arrive at a new address. The paperwork changes, the phone number changes, the morning greetings change. But the heart doesn’t arrive that day. Years later you still read the news from the city you left before anything else each evening, you still dream in the old streets, and on a truly sad day there is no one at the new address you can call. Anyone who has lived with the body in one place and the heart in another knows. Arriving and settling are different things. Arriving ends in a day. No one can say when settling ends.

The time of a person wearing the mask is exactly this. The business cards came, the nameplate was hung, the graduation gown was put on. On paper, the move into the name is finished. But the inner person hasn’t settled there. A house where the name has arrived and the person hasn’t yet moved in: put into everyday words, that is about what “impostor syndrome” means. We expect settling to happen on its own with time, but as we saw, the years tend to widen the gap. Settling, it seems, is not something time does. So what makes a person put down roots inside one name?

Psychology weighed the mask, and the weighing was accurate. But it cannot say who will receive the face underneath. The diagnosis is precise and the prescription is right, and the last line, who receives that bare face, is empty. That blank is no neglect on psychology’s part. It is not a blank psychology can fill. It is a matter of promise: someone who knows me completely and still says, I want you. Is there such a someone? When a person puts down the invented name and speaks the true one, is there a place in this world where that name meets welcome and not a verdict?

One person faced that question not as an idea but with his life. A small city in the north of France, a man’s room. On the mantel stand two silver candlesticks, and in front of them a man has been pacing all night. He is the one whose voice we heard in the opening. At daybreak he will have to choose between two names. A splendid false one that everyone loves, and a true one that will bring everything down the moment he says it.

One Man's Story · Champmathieu's Night

To understand that sleepless night, we have to go back first to an evening eight years earlier: the evening he left Digne with two candlesticks in his arms.

Valjean walked through the fields all that day. He didn’t know where he was going. His head was in a tangle. For nineteen years he had learned how to handle hatred: how to take a beating, how to swallow an insult, how to hold on with the whole world set against him. Those skills had kept him alive. But this morning’s event could not be handled with any of them. The gendarmes would have been easier, because he knew how to deal with a gendarme. He had no idea how to deal with that bishop. A man he could not hate, could not run from, could not repay. In the world his nineteen years had taught him, there was no place for such a person to live, and that world was shaking beneath his feet. The whole world had called him a beast, and he had survived by accepting the verdict. A beast has a peace of its own: expecting nothing, owing nothing. Then one man overturned the verdict. Against the voices of the whole world, a single man said the opposite word. Brother. Now Valjean had to choose. Either the whole world was right and the bishop was wrong, or the whole world was wrong and the bishop was right. The farther he walked, the closer the question followed.

Toward sunset he sat down to rest beside a bush in the field. A song drifted from far off. A boy of about ten came walking along the road, a chimney sweep from Savoy. His face was smudged with soot, but his voice was bright. He was tossing the few coins from his day’s earnings into the air and catching them, humming a song from his home country. He could even catch one on the back of his hand and toss it again. Only a child plays with everything he owns in the air. Then one coin slipped past his hand. A forty-sou piece, silver: for a chimney sweep, several days’ work. It hit the ground and rolled, and it rolled right up to where Valjean sat.

Valjean’s foot came down on it.

The foot moved before the thought did. It was a reflex that nineteen years had carved into his body. Whatever rolls in front of you, cover it. Take possession before it can be taken. The rule prison had taught him came out through his foot without passing through thought.

The boy ran up. “Sir, that’s my money. Please take your foot off it.” Valjean did not answer. He was looking into the distance like a man who could not hear. The boy tried again. “It’s mine. It’s forty sous. I’m Petit Gervais. Please, give me my money back.” Still no answer. When the boy took hold of Valjean’s knee and began to cry, a low, rough sound came out of Valjean’s mouth. “Get out.” The boy was terrified. He pointed at the foot once more and begged, but when Valjean made as if to rise, he burst into tears and ran. The small footsteps faded, and the field was empty again.

Valjean sat there a long while. The sun touched the horizon and the wind turned cold. When he moved to stand, something glinted beneath his foot. The silver coin, catching the evening light.

He looked down at it. At first he seemed like a man who did not recognize what it was. Then he jerked back as though he had been struck by lightning. The morning, and what had happened a moment before, fell on him all at once. The man who had taken the silver candlesticks in both hands that morning had pinned a child’s coin under his boot before the sun was down. The hands that had been given a life as a gift had taken a crying child’s day of work. The bishop had called him brother, and he had made an animal’s sound at a boy.

Valjean snatched up the coin and ran in the direction the boy had gone. Petit Gervais. He ran shouting the name the boy had left behind. Petit Gervais! Petit Gervais! The field gave no answer. At every fork he stopped and shouted, he climbed a rise and scanned in every direction, he grabbed passersby and asked whether they had seen a chimney sweep. No one had. He stopped a priest riding by on horseback, pressed money into his hand, and begged him, in a rush of half-sentences, to give it to the poor, and if he ever met a boy who swept chimneys, to find him, please. The priest rode off with no idea what had happened. Darkness fell. Valjean’s voice grew stranger and stranger. At first it was calling. By the end it was closer to crying.

At last Valjean stopped. He collapsed where he stood, put his face in his hands, and wept.

It was his first weeping in nineteen years. He had not wept on the day he was sentenced to five years for a loaf of bread. He had not wept when he was beaten, or when he was called by a number, or when even a doghouse was closed to him. Hatred had carried him through all of it, and hatred freezes tears. Now, in this field where no one was beating him, the ice was breaking. A prison could not break this man, nor could the lash. Two candlesticks were breaking him. More exactly, it was his own face in the candlelight. That the man called brother and the man who had trodden on a child’s coin were the same man: anyone who sees both faces at once has no choice but to weep.

In the dark, a groan escaped his lips.

I am a wretch (Je suis un misérable).

How long he wept like that, no one knows. But one thing has been handed down. Out in the open field, a man who had gone nineteen years without a tear dropped to the ground and wept, and the dark held him there. He had the bishop’s silver and the bishop’s words in his head, and a coin under his boot that was not his.

The bishop’s door, that night too, was not locked.

Where that night led, no one in Digne ever learned. The man with the yellow passport vanished that same day, and some time later a stranger with empty hands appeared in a city in the north. What name he gave himself and what eight years he built, you have heard. Then came the sleepless night, and the end of that night was this.

Before daybreak he climbed into a carriage.

Arras was a full day’s ride away. Mayor Madeleine had not left with a decision made. He couldn’t decide either way, so he started by moving his body. Let me go and see. I’ll decide after I’ve seen. The road kept blocking him. A wheel broke. There was no carriage to switch to. The man who could mend the wheel, they said, would not be free until tomorrow. Each time an obstacle appeared, some corner of his heart was secretly glad. So this is as far as I go. The road is closed. Yet his hands, before he knew it, were finding a new wheel, hiring another carriage, urging the horses on into the night. A man whose heart says turn back and whose body keeps going forward. Split that way, he rode all day, and only after dark did he stand at the door of the court at Arras.

The courtroom was packed. His rank as mayor ushered him to a seat of honor behind the judges. From there he saw the man in the dock for the first time.

An elderly man sat there, bewildered. His name was Champmathieu, and he said he had spent his life mending cartwheels. The moment the mayor saw that face, a dizziness came over him. The stooped shoulders, the roughened hands, the forehead pressed down by the years: it felt as if an aged version of himself were looking back at him from an old mirror. The man who had once stared at a stranger’s face in a puddle now met his own face in a courtroom dock. He did not need to ask the mirror who belonged in that seat. It was already telling him.

The charge was apple theft: he had broken off apples from a branch that hung over an orchard wall. That alone would have been a matter of a few months. What tangled everything was his face. The prosecutor stood and named the man’s true identity. The defendant was not Champmathieu but Jean Valjean. A convict who had served nineteen years, a dangerous man who had broken the terms of his parole and disappeared. Three witnesses were called: Brevet, Chenildieu, Cochepaille. Convicts who had spent a season with Valjean in the prison at Toulon looked the defendant over one after another and said the same thing. Yes, it’s Jean Valjean. He’s older, but that is the face.

Then the prosecutor turned over one more page. The defendant Valjean had one further charge outstanding. Eight years earlier, in a field near Digne, he had robbed a young chimney sweep of his money. The name of the victim was read aloud. Petit Gervais.

Behind the judges, the mayor’s breath stopped at that name. He knew it. It was the name he had shouted in a sunset field until his throat tore, the name he had called and called until it turned into weeping. The one silver coin that had brought him to his knees had followed him for eight years and arrived here. Carried on a clerk’s dry voice, it had become a line in an indictment. What he thought had ended in tears had come back as a sentence. Highway robbery, and a repeat offense. If this charge held, the penalty could reach his life.

In the dock, Champmathieu still did not understand. I only picked up some apples. They were lying on the ground. I don’t know any Jean Valjean. I’ve mended wheels all my life. His words came out in pieces, and in pieces they sounded absurd, and laughter leaked out here and there in the gallery. No one believes the words of a man who cannot prove his own name. The trial was effectively over. Only the sentence remained.

In the seat of honor behind the bench, the mayor was weighing two outcomes on a scale.

If he stayed silent, it would go like this. Madeleine would be safe forever. The name Jean Valjean would pass to that man today and be buried with him in prison. The factory would keep running, hundreds of families’ dinner tables would be protected, the hospital would stand and the school would stand. There was only one loss: the remaining years of a stranger in the dock. If he spoke, it would go like this. One man would go home. And Madeleine would vanish. The factory would shake, the workers would scatter, and all the good built up over eight years would come down with it. Which saved more people? The sum looked obvious. One man against hundreds. From deep inside, a whisper rose. Perhaps this is the way God is tidying things up. I can serve more people by keeping silent. Little is as quiet and as strong as the reason that says you are doing it to keep doing good. Somewhere right now, in some boardroom or group chat, someone is working out the same sum. Silence always comes with a plausible reason.

Then the judge asked the defendant whether he had anything last to say. Weeping, the defendant repeated himself. “I’m Champmathieu. It’s true. I’m Champmathieu.”

As that voice, begging for his own name back, rang through the room, a man seated behind the bench stood up.

“Release the defendant. That man is not Jean Valjean.”

The room fell silent. The judge, the prosecutor, the gendarmes: all recognized the man on his feet. The respected mayor, the best-known name in the region. “Monsieur Mayor,” the judge asked, “what are you saying?” He walked to the middle of the courtroom. Then, quietly, he said:

“I am Jean Valjean.”

No one laughed. But no one believed him either. The judge assumed overwork had clouded his mind, and the prosecutor looked at him with pity. So he had to prove it. In the very place where others strain to prove their innocence, he had to prove his guilt. He turned to the three convicts in the witness box. Brevet, in prison you wore checkered knit suspenders. Chenildieu, you have a deep burn scar on your shoulder, from pressing it into a brazier of coals to erase the brand. Cochepaille, on your left arm there is a date tattooed in blue. March 1, 1815. Roll up your sleeve.

Cochepaille rolled up his sleeve. A gendarme brought a lamp close. The date was there.

The courtroom froze where it was. Those who were present would later remember that moment as a strange silence in which no one could tell who was judging and who was being judged. In that silence he spoke. “Send that man home. And if you wish to take me, come whenever you like. I am not running.” No one stopped him. He walked out through the open door.

For nineteen years the world had taken his name from him, and for eight years he had hidden it himself. A moment ago, in the most dangerous place in the world, he had taken it back with his own mouth. The world had taken his name and he had hidden it; only he could say it aloud again. The freest man in that courtroom that day was the one who had pronounced his own sentence.

Soon after, Valjean was arrested. The law added the charge of that single silver coin and sentenced him to death, then reduced it to life at hard labor. He put on the convict’s clothes again and went back to the prison at Toulon. On his chest, instead of his name, was a new number. 9430. The same world that had fastened 24601 on a young man of twenty-five or so now fastened 9430 on him, white-haired. It was a name he had taken back at the risk of his life. It took the world only a few months to turn it into a number again.

Late that autumn, on a warship in Toulon harbor, a white-haired convict climbed up to save a sailor hanging high in the rigging. Then, on the way down, he fell into the sea. The records read: convict 9430, dead. That a man had swum ashore under cover of that night, the records had no way of knowing. Officially dead, Valjean went to keep a promise. A woman who had worked in his factory and died of illness had called, to the end, the name of her little daughter. He went to find the girl, Cosette, and take her in. A child with no parents and a man hiding his name became each other’s family. Years passed. The girl grew, the man grew old, and Javert never gave up.

His Story · Two Charcoal Fires

The story pauses here to catch its breath. A man who risked his life to take back his name: how does he live by that name now? The footsteps behind him have not stopped, because Javert has not given up, but we will set the end of that pursuit aside for a while. Scripture does not answer this question with a lecture. It shows us a person instead. A fisherman from the Sea of Galilee, a man named Simon.

Depart From Me

From the very beginning, he tried to run.

He had thrown his nets all night and caught nothing. He was at the water’s edge washing them when a stranger, a teacher, asked to borrow his boat. When the teaching was over, the teacher told him to go out to the deep water and let down his nets. To a fisherman, it was a laughable instruction. After a night of nothing, in broad daylight, in deep water instead of shallows? Simon said he had toiled all night and caught nothing, and still, on the strength of the teacher’s word, he let the nets down. Fish came up until the nets began to tear. When both boats were close to sinking, Simon did not count the catch. He fell to his knees. “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord” (Luke 5:8, ESV).

Faced with the biggest catch of his life, he begged the teacher to leave instead of rejoicing. We already know this reaction. The man running with a bag of silver knew it. When we stand before something cleaner than we are, we instinctively widen the distance, because the nearer the light, the darker our shadow looks. Simon’s first prayer was not come closer but go away, and that shows us where all of us begin.

Jesus did not leave. He told Simon not to be afraid, and said that from now on he would be catching people (see Luke 5:10). The confession of sin did not disqualify him. It became the place where the calling began. From the start, Jesus had a name for this fisherman: Cephas, an Aramaic word meaning rock, which Greek renders Peter (see John 1:42). Later he confirmed it. “You are Peter” (Matt 16:18, ESV).

That naming was, by any measure, outrageous. Simon was about as far from a rock as a man could be. Quick with words, quick to be afraid, bigger in promises than in follow-through. Of the twelve, he may have been the one who wavered most, and he was handed the name of the thing that does not waver. The name had grown far ahead of the man. Worldly titles come after the results. This name came before any, as a promise. Jesus did not hire him from a résumé. He gave the name first and then raised the man toward it. Like the name the couple in the first story kept on the refrigerator door and called aloud, this was a name where welcome arrived before achievement.

The Fire in the Courtyard

That name looked farthest away on one particular night, so let us begin there.

At the last supper, Jesus told Simon ahead of time that before the rooster crowed that night, he would deny knowing him three times. Simon bristled. Even if everyone else stumbled, he would not, he insisted. With Jesus he would go to prison and to death. He probably meant it. His sincerity was real. What he lacked was anything solid underneath it.

That night Jesus was arrested, and Simon followed at a distance as far as the courtyard of the high priest’s house. It was spring, but the night air was cold, and the servants and officers had made a charcoal fire in the middle of the courtyard. Simon slipped in among them and warmed himself (see John 18:18). His face was lit by the fire, and he hoped no one would recognize him. The servant girl who kept the door looked into his face and asked whether he too was one of that man’s disciples. Simon said no. He said he did not know the man. Two more questions came, and two more denials went out. The third time he even swore with a curse. It was not in front of soldiers with drawn swords. It began with a doorkeeper’s question and ended among people standing around a fire. What broke him was not torture but the terror of being found out.

Then the rooster crowed. Luke recorded a sentence no one forgets. “And the Lord turned and looked at Peter” (Luke 22:61, ESV). Jesus, being led away bound, had turned and looked at Simon across the courtyard. What was in that look, Scripture does not explain. It records only what happened where the look landed. Simon could not stay where he was. “And he went out and wept bitterly” (Luke 22:62, ESV).

On that night a man named Rock fell apart three times, beginning with a single question from a doorkeeper. The gap between a name received and a life lived could not be wider. If Simon learned anything that night, it was the bottom of his own sincerity. But there is one thing worth holding on to. He wept. Like the man who wept in the field with a silver coin in his hand, he wept where he had fallen, as he had fallen. He did not try to balance the books again. He did not try to punish himself. A broken man weeping, still broken: that was all Simon did that night, and we will soon see that it was enough.

The Fire in the Morning

He had already seen the risen Lord. Even so, Simon went back to Galilee and told his companions, “I am going fishing” (John 21:3, ESV). A failed man goes back to the old place, to the boat and nets he had left three years before. As if he had left the name Rock behind in the courtyard that night, he went out again in the night boat as Simon the fisherman. And he caught nothing all night. Like the night they first met.

Toward dawn, someone stood on the shore. At his word the nets went down on the right side of the boat, and they were so full that the men could not haul them in. Then one disciple recognized him: it is the Lord. The moment Simon heard it, he jumped into the water. The man who at their first meeting had begged Jesus to leave could not wait for the boat to land, and made his way through the water toward him. Same lake, same empty night, same miracle. Only the direction of the man had changed.

When they came ashore, a charcoal fire was already burning, with fish on it, and bread. A charcoal fire. The last time Simon had stood beside one was the high priest’s courtyard, the night he threw away his own name three times by the firelight. The same kind of light was now warming a breakfast. It is too exact to call a coincidence. One way to read it is that Jesus called him back under the very lighting where he had lost his name, to give it back in that very place. If comfort is a way around the place of shame, restoration is going back to the place of shame together and laying a new memory over it.

After breakfast Jesus spoke. “Simon, son of John, do you love me more than these?” (John 21:15, ESV). Not Peter, but Simon: the name of the day they first met. To a man who had gone back to his old place because the big name embarrassed him, Jesus spoke in his first name, as if he knew the embarrassment. It was a call that said, you can begin again from here. The question came three times. Do you love me? At the third, Scripture says, Peter was grieved. He could not have missed what three meant. A man who had denied three times in the courtyard was given three places to say he loved. The denials were not erased. Three confessions were laid over them. At the third question Peter answered, “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you” (John 21:17, ESV). You know everything. In those words is the calm of a man with nothing left to be found out. The lifelong dread of the one wearing the mask is being entirely known. Simon was speaking from the place of being entirely known. The one who had heard every word he said that night, including I do not know him, had not left, and still asked for his love. Found out, and not thrown away. The question we asked, whether there are arms to receive the unmasked face, finds its answer here.

Each answer was followed by a word. Feed my lambs. Tend my sheep. Feed my sheep (see John 21:15–17). Jesus never once brought up that night. There was no settling of accounts. Instead he handed over his own flock. Restoration did not end with a pardon. It was completed with a commission. The world forgives a wrongdoer and puts him in the back row. Jesus handed the one who failed the thing he cared about most. It was the same at Digne. Breakfast instead of interrogation, entrusting instead of accounting.

The Man Who Became Shade

Fifty days after the resurrection, on the festival of Pentecost, an astonishing scene took place in the middle of Jerusalem. The man who had fallen apart three times beside the courtyard fire stood before crowds of thousands and proclaimed the very name he had denied. The record says about three thousand people joined them that day. A little later, the scene was this. People carried the sick out into the streets on their beds and laid them in rows, hoping that when Peter came by, “at least his shadow might fall on some of them” (Acts 5:15, ESV).

A shadow. In the high priest’s courtyard he had tried to wipe out his name in the light of the fire. Now people pushed the sick under his shadow. The man who hid his name had become a man who casts shade.

Among the parables Jesus told about the kingdom of God is the mustard seed. A seed “the smallest of all the seeds on earth” is sown, and “when it is sown it grows up and becomes larger than all the garden plants and puts out large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade” (Mark 4:31–32, ESV). The completion of a mustard tree is its shade, a place where others come to rest. It is a tree something can nest in. What that kingdom looks like when it grows inside one person, Simon’s life shows. The seed that denied its teacher three times beside the courtyard fire became a tree that did not waver even under the threats of the Sanhedrin, the high council of the day, and sheltered the sick in its shade.

The Cut Rope

Having come through the morning of the charcoal fire, we can go back to the footsteps we set aside. We have to see where that pursuit ended before we can ask the last question.

Gunfire rang out in Paris one early summer of that time. Barricades went up in the heart of the city. Inside stood Marius, the young man Cosette loved, and Valjean had gone in to protect him. Tied to a post inside the barricade was a captured spy. It was Javert. The rebels had decided that if the battle turned against them they would execute the spy, and Valjean stepped forward. “Let me take care of that man.” Given the devotion he had shown, they agreed. Valjean led Javert into a lane behind the barricade. Javert did not close his eyes. “If you’re going to do it, do it quickly. Dying at your hands is a fitting end.” In reply Valjean took out a pocketknife. For the first time, Javert’s eyes wavered. The blade came down not on his throat but on the rope.

“Go. You are free.”

Javert did not move. Valjean went on.

“When this is over I will be at number 7, Rue de l’Homme-Armé. If you want to arrest me, come there.”

Javert looked at him for a long moment, then spoke.

“I don’t change. The next time we meet, I will arrest you.”

“As you wish,” said Valjean.

Javert disappeared into the darkness of the lane. Valjean fired a single shot into the air, went back, and said only that it was done.

He had held in his hand the hunter who had chased him for years, and let him go. With no conditions, and without receiving so much as a word of thanks. We know where we have seen that gesture. It is the hand that added the two candlesticks for the man who had stolen. The one who had received was now the one who gave.

The barricade fell. The young man was shot and collapsed, and Valjean put him on his back. Then he lifted an iron cover in the street and went down into the belly of Paris. The place where all the filth of the city gathers and flows, a labyrinth no light had ever entered. With another’s life on his shoulder, he walked for hours in darkness up to his waist. Where the ground gave way in the mire, he held the young man over his head and crossed with the filth up to his chin. Medals are pinned on in bright rooms, but proofs of love are often made in the dark. A man past sixty carried a man of barely twenty along that path, and the map of Paris still calls it a sewer, nothing more.

When the iron grate at the exit swung open and he came out onto the riverbank in the evening, a man stood on the embankment. It was Javert.

Valjean was not surprised. “Inspector. Good. I am yours now. I have one request. Let me take this young man home first.” Javert looked from the young man on his back to the man standing there soaked in filth. Then he gave an answer he could not himself explain. “I’ll call a carriage.”

After the young man had been handed over to his family, Valjean made one last request. Let me stop at my home for a moment. There are things to settle. In front of number 7, Rue de l’Homme-Armé, Javert said, “Go up. I’ll wait here.” Valjean climbed the stairs, and when he looked down at the street from the window, it was empty. Javert had gone. He had left the prey he had never once let slip, standing at the door.

Javert walked the night streets for a long time.

His world had been, all his life, a single straight line. There was the law, and there were those who kept it and those who broke it. A convict reoffends: he had staked his life on that one sentence. Yet tonight the man who spared his life was the very convict he had hunted all his life. Such a convict appeared nowhere in his rulebook.

Two roads now lay before him, and he could take neither. He could not arrest the man to whom he owed his life, and he could not, as a man of the law, let him go. Yet he had already let him go. A man who had never received what he could not repay had received it for the first time in his life.

He stopped at a nearby guard post and wrote a few lines on paper. A few weak points in how prisoners were transported, a few proposals for improving jail regulations. Until the last night he was a man of the rules. And that night, he never came back.

Two men received the same thing: a life given back for nothing, in a place where death was deserved. Valjean received it, wept on the ground like a man waking up, took back his name, and became a man who carries others. Javert received it and wandered the night streets, looking for somewhere to return it.

What happened deep in Javert’s heart that night, only God knows. Passing sentence on a person’s last night is not ours to do. But that night leaves a question for us, the readers of this book. When I have received what I cannot repay, where do I put it?

As the Name Received

Peter beside the charcoal fire had been sure of himself, and he lived to see that certainty collapse in a single night. The boast that even if all the others fell he would not shattered at the courtyard fire. Yet what he did after falling was not to balance the books again. He wept, broken, and then sat down at the breakfast table that came to the place where he wept. That is what receiving looks like. Empty-handed, broken, sitting at a table already set.

But to say only this is to give half an answer. Where does the strength to receive come from? Who supplies the power to weep, to endure broken, to hold out empty hands? If even that is a matter of willpower, we are back at the beginning. Here Scripture brings out one of its deepest promises, given through the prophet Ezekiel. “And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh” (Ezek 36:26, ESV). The Hebrew picture is more vivid still: a heart of stone (lev ha-even) taken out, a heart of flesh (lev basar) put in. This is a heart transplant. The subject is God from start to finish: I will give, I will put, I will remove, I will give. There is not one verb for us to perform. Becoming a new person is not the product of resolve but the result of surgery, and the patient’s only task is to lie down on the table. Who performs the surgery? Ezekiel says in the very next verse: “And I will put my Spirit within you” (Ezek 36:27, ESV). God makes the new heart by placing his own Spirit within a person. The New Testament calls him the Holy Spirit. The one who blew like the wind in the third story and opened the door now stays within and turns the heart of stone to a heart of flesh.

If we boil down what was settled that morning on the Galilean shore into one sentence, it is this. A status received is not canceled by failure, and the way we live grows out of that status. The order matters. We do not grow in order to become children. We grow because we are children. The root is the name, and the fruit is the life. (The church has an older word for this slow growth, sanctification: the lifelong process by which a child of God grows to fit the name.) Jesus once spoke of this order with a picture of a bath. “The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet, but is completely clean” (John 13:10, ESV). Over a day’s walking the feet pick up plenty of dust, but dust on the feet does not cancel the bath. On the evening of a fall, the task is not to take the whole bath again but to wash the feet and sit back down at the table. That is exactly what the morning by the charcoal fire did for Peter.

Revelation 3:8, which opens this fourth story, is a line from a letter the risen Lord sent to a small church in Philadelphia, a city in what is now western Turkey. The letter introduces him as the one “who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut, who shuts and no one opens” (Rev 3:7, ESV), and goes on: “Behold, I have set before you an open door, which no one is able to shut. I know that you have but little power, and yet you have kept my word and have not denied my name” (Rev 3:8, ESV). The open door was first a path laid before that small church. But the praise attached to it stays in the mind: with little power, they had not denied his name. Think of the man who denied his teacher’s name three times beside the fire, and the weight of that praise is clear. Not great power but little, and still not leaving that name: this is what living by the name received means. The same letter ends with this promise. “The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it, and I will write on him the name of my God, and the name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down from my God out of heaven, and my own new name” (Rev 3:12, ESV). As the wavering fisherman became a rock, a wavering person becomes a pillar, and a new name is written on it. The door no one can shut, which the third story promised, is this one. An open door is an invitation to come in, and a new name is the promise that the one who has come in will not be sent out again.

Then where does the strength to grow on that status come from? At the word growth we reach for resolutions, though we know a New Year’s resolution rarely survives February. The parable Jesus told about growth is comforting for that reason.

After sowing, the farmer sleeps and rises. No farmer stays up all night pulling on the sprouts, because he knows the power that grows them lies not in his diligence but in the life inside the seed. By itself: that is the adverb Scripture attaches to growth. The parable first tells how the kingdom of God grows, but the same principle runs through the life of one person planted in that kingdom. A new person comes from surgery, and in the same way growth comes from life. Our part is not to pull on the sprout but to plant, sleep, and wake. The body knows something similar. Muscles are said to grow not while we exercise but while we rest and sleep, because the body fills the tiny tears exercise made a little thicker than before. The moment of effort and the moment of growth are not the same.

But an honest question arises here. If life grows by itself, why does some faith stay the same size for years? A potted plant on a balcony knows the answer. A tree the same height for years, watered, in the sun, and not growing. Turn the pot over and the reason shows. The roots circle the inside wall, packed tight. No birds nest in that tree as they did in the mustard tree, because it never had room to grow into shade. The seed held the whole design for growing toward the sky. The tree is small not from any limit in the seed but from the size of the pot. A life that does not grow is usually short of room, not short of life. The roots have hit a wall.

A borrowed name is a shallow pot. Madeleine was a valuable, splendid pot, but somewhere along the way the roots had touched the wall. That is what the man who couldn’t sleep on the night of the opening dimly sensed. What broke on the day he stood up in the courtroom and said I am Jean Valjean was the pot, and the years of prison and flight that followed may have been the first season in which his roots took hold of the ground. They say a potted tree moved into the ground starts growing again. But in the first season it looks worse. Leaves fall, branches droop, and for some weeks you wonder whether the move was a mistake. The roots are busy gripping the new soil and have no strength to send upward. The time after believing when nothing seemed to change, or when things seemed even shakier, may be that kind of time. Above, nothing seems different. Below, everything is changing. The problem was never the tree but the pot, and now the tree is in the ground.

Jesus spoke of the secret of growth in the language of staying attached, not effort. “I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing” (John 15:5, ESV). A branch does not squeeze out its fruit. It stays on the vine, and fruit comes as the result of that union. What soil does for a planted tree, the Word and prayer do for the one who stays attached, and so do worship, baptism, the Lord’s Supper, and the people around us. Life planted where its roots can spread freely grows even while we sleep and wake. What made Simon a rock was not Simon’s resolve but an unbroken connection to the one who came back to him three times after he fell three times.

None of this leaves us sitting as spectators. Paul wrote of the same grace this way: “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure” (Phil 2:12–13, ESV). The call to work and the declaration that God is the one at work stand side by side in a single sentence. In True Spirituality, Francis Schaeffer described this posture as an active passivity, which we might also call active surrender. A picture of it is Mary before the angel’s news. “Behold, I am the servant of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word” (Luke 1:38, ESV). Mary did not make the child by her own strength. But she gave her body and her life over to the promise as actively as anyone could. Fighting sin is the same. Paul wrote, “if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live” (Rom 8:13, ESV). The fight is real, and we are the ones who fight it, but the power that wins it belongs to the Spirit who has come to live within us. Schaeffer also cautioned against shrinking the Christian life to a handful of taboos. At its center, he said, is the law of love for God and for neighbor. Obedience is not the price paid for grace but the gratitude of someone who has received it. Even that grateful response is something God uses. Sitting down before the Word, praying, taking part in baptism and the Lord’s Supper, loving the person beside us: these are not labor to squeeze out fruit. They are responses of love, choosing day by day to stay attached.

In the first story we folded away one question: not what have I accomplished, but who signed me. Now we unfold it. Paul wrote: “For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them” (Eph 2:10, ESV). The Greek word behind “workmanship” is poiēma, a work of art, and our word “poem” comes from it. We saw in the refrigerator picture of the first story that a work’s value is set by the signature, not the material. One clumsy name in the corner made that paper impossible to trade for anything. On the signature line of the work that is you, there is the name of the one who made you. And look at the order of this verse. First created as a work of art, then good works. Being comes first and doing after. We do not become a work of art by doing good. Good works flow out because we are a work of art. The whole road we have traveled across four stories is contained in the order of this one verse.

Then there is the other question folded away in the first story, ready to be unfolded. In a room made with this much care, why does living hurt so often? The second story said that the pain did not begin with my failures; it began when the guest walked away from the spring. The third story said that the one who built the room paid the price of sin at the very place where the pain began, and carried even our sorrows in his own body. Two pieces remain.

First, this pain has an end. It is the day Christ returns and sets everything right. Scripture says it is appointed for people to die once, and after that comes judgment (see Heb 9:27). On that day we all stand before the judgment seat of Christ (see 2 Cor 5:10). To stand there apart from him is to stand alone, with my debt mine to pay. That is why the gospel invites us today, before it speaks of that day. And we already know who will sit on that judgment seat. He is the one who took the judgment in our place, who lit a charcoal fire and called a broken man by his first name again. He received the judgment that was mine, and his righteousness became mine. So for the person in him, that day is not a day of waiting for a verdict. It is the day the verdict already received, no condemnation (see Rom 8:1), is confirmed before the whole world. That is why Scripture speaks of his return not in the language of fear but of waiting. Christ “will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him” (Heb 9:28, ESV). The last book of the Bible paints the scene of that day: the day when “the dwelling place of God is with man” (Rev 21:3, ESV), the day the owner of this room stays at his guests’ side himself.

Nor pain anymore. To someone who has lost a loved one, what Scripture says first is not an explanation but this promise. There is nothing shameful in grief. Before it is put to any use, it is a tear that needs wiping. Then the one seated on the throne adds, “Behold, I am making all things new” (Rev 21:5, ESV). That is not a word about discarding this room and building another. It is about making this room new. The room that was so lavishly furnished in the first chapter of Genesis becomes a home again in the last scene of the Bible. The words Jacob cried out when he woke on his stone pillow at Bethel, “This is none other than the house of God” (Gen 28:17, ESV), become at last the confession of the whole world.

The other piece concerns the time of waiting. Pain that has been lived through is not thrown away. The man who hid his name in the high priest’s courtyard cast a shadow over the sick, and nineteen years of chains became a hand that cuts another man’s rope. No pain is wasted. So until that day we live as shade. Toward the day the pain ends, as a tree where the hurting can nest.

Finally, think of the man in the courtroom. He gave up a splendid false name and spoke a dangerous true one with his own mouth. He could do it not because his courage was exceptional but because he had already heard another voice call that name. It was the bishop’s voice, calling him brother while the world called him 24601, It was the voice that pointed past itself to the one who, beside the courtyard fire, called a fallen man back with Simon, son of John. We heard it already in the verdict of the third story (Rom 8:1). For the person in Christ, the last word does not belong to the accusation. Someone standing on that verdict can stand without a mask.

The first story began like this: God counts you weighty. The last story closes like this: that weight has a name on it. You were not mixed together by accident; you are a work with a signature. You are not someone who has to earn a name with results but someone who receives the name he holds out first. That name is set before you now. For the one who receives it, it is not canceled even on the most shameful night, and every morning by the charcoal fire you are called by it again. One thing remains for the one who receives: to grow into the name received. May the story of a man who ran and became a man who takes others in become yours.

My Story — Before You Close the Book

We have come through the fourth and last story. The first story showed how preciously we were made. The second showed how deeply we were lost. The third showed the price paid to bring the lost one home. The fourth was about how someone bought with a price lives afterward. The strength to take off the mask comes from arms that will receive us. Growth comes from connection, not resolve. A life that does not grow is usually short of room. And the question of pain, which we have carried since the first story, has a promise waiting at its end: the day every tear is wiped away. Now let’s pause and bring this story home.

Closing

Two Candles

I owe you the end of the story.

Years passed, and Jean Valjean spent his last night in a small room in Paris. On the mantel, two silver candlesticks were burning. They had come with him from the table at Digne to his knapsack, from the knapsack to the mayor’s fireplace, and through the courtroom, the barricade, and the sewer, all the way to his bedside. Cosette, the girl he had raised as his own daughter, sat with him, and so did her husband, Marius. He told them about her mother. Then he left them one request: love one another. There is little else in this world, he said, that is worth much. He turned his eyes to the candles and added, quietly, that he wondered whether the one who had given them was watching from heaven and glad. He had done what he could. In that light he slipped away as if falling asleep.

His grave carries no name. In a corner of the Père Lachaise cemetery lies a single stone with nothing carved on it. Someone once wrote four lines of verse on it in pencil, Hugo tells us, and even those have been washed away by rain and time. A man who spent his life fighting over a name now lies under a stone without one. As the last sentence of a story, that may sound bleak. I believe it is the truest full stop this story could reach, because his name no longer needed to be cut into stone to be kept. Jesus told his followers to “rejoice that your names are written in heaven” (Luke 10:20, ESV). A name carved in stone wears away. A name written in heaven does not. Valjean wasn’t buried having lost his name. It looks more as if he left it in the safest place there is.

We have walked through four stories together. A guest who was awaited. Numbers that erased people. A pair of candlesticks handed over for nothing. A name given back. From creation to the fall, from redemption to new life, these four were one long sentence spelled out slowly: God counts you weighty. That weight is what made us, what ached when we were lost, what paid our price, and what gave us our names back.

The gospel works in a life in the same order every time. First it empties the hands, then it places a name in them. Where the things we gripped to prove ourselves have slipped away, a name we did not earn is laid down. The life that follows is not a life of guarding that name, because Someone else guards it to the end. The life that follows is a life of growing into it.

This book was written to keep company with four stories about meeting the gospel. The story of a person who has heard them does not end here. The road runs through the water of baptism to a church’s table, and it becomes a lifelong road of growing with Jesus. I hope this book has been one stepping stone along the way, on the road where the story of Jesus becomes your story, whether at a small group’s table or alone at your desk.

The door of Myriel’s house was not locked that night. The door that one pointed to is not locked now either. In Jesus’ story, a place for your name has already been prepared. The morning beside the charcoal fire is waiting for your name too. The prayer at the end of the third story, “A Prayer in Answer to the Invitation,” is still right where it was. This door is never locked.
